# Robotics

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

Robotics studies machines that sense, compute, and act in the physical world, together with the cultural, economic, political, and human systems that give those machines purpose and meaning. Full Notes use the focused child topics below rather than this chapter tag directly.

## Overview Chapter

Robotics is not one technology with a settled boundary. It is a meeting point for mechanics, sensing, computation, artificial intelligence, materials, communication, design, and institutions. A robot changes physical reality, so errors and choices that might remain informational in ordinary software can become motion, force, surveillance, care, transport, or violence. Understanding the field therefore requires both its internal architecture and the human arrangements that select goals, allocate authority, absorb risk, and interpret machine behavior.

[[Robotics History and Cultural Imagination]] explains why discussion begins with stories as much as engineering. Mechanical automata performed artificial life centuries before electronics, while R.U.R. embedded forced labor in the very word robot. Asimov's fictional laws, Western rebellion narratives, Japanese traditions of benevolent robot characters, and the blurred categories of robot, android, and cyborg shaped public expectations before practical systems existed. These narratives remain active design constraints because they influence what people trust, fear, fund, regulate, and mistake for intelligence.

[[Robot Architecture and Autonomy]] follows the physical feedback loop that distinguishes robotics from disembodied computation. A platform needs a suitable structure and locomotion system, sensors for both the environment and its own condition, processing that can tolerate noise and delay, and actuators governed by layered control. Some machines build plans and maps; others use short behavior-based loops whose local reactions produce coherent activity. Path planning, swarm coordination, cloud resources, and components borrowed from larger industries extend capability, but each also creates new dependencies and failure modes.

[[Autonomous Vehicle Systems and Society]] applies those mechanisms to an infrastructure already central to modern life. Self-driving vehicles combine cameras, radar, lidar, positioning, vehicle-pose measurements, maps, and control, while developers can pursue a clean software-centered platform or incrementally integrate existing driver-assistance systems. Platoons and shared autonomous fleets promise better utilization and traffic flow, yet liability, public perception of machine risk, software failure, pricing, land use, insurance, employment, and incumbent resistance determine whether technical performance becomes durable mobility.

[[Military Robotics and Autonomous Weapons]] shows how strategic objectives reshape the same technologies. Unmanned air, sea, and ground systems extend reach, multiply force, reduce some risks to soldiers, and perform surveillance or bomb disposal in dangerous environments. Weapon autonomy creates a critical distinction among humans who authorize every shot, supervise and can override a machine, or remain outside the decision loop. Communications, jamming, capture, operator psychology, accountability, and the political distance created by remote force make military robotics inseparable from ethics and international order.

[[Robotics Labor and Political Economy]] examines how robots reorganize work rather than simply counting machines against jobs. Automation occurs at the task level and depends on codification, labor costs, capital costs, process redesign, and complementary human work. Collaborative factory robots and warehouse systems illustrate divisions of labor in which people supply perception and dexterity while machines provide endurance, movement, and repetition. Productivity gains can still flow disproportionately to owners, while displaced workers face skill, geographic, and institutional barriers that aggregate employment statistics conceal.

[[Human-Robot Interaction and Augmentation]] treats a robot and the surrounding people as one work system. Search-and-rescue operations reveal that setup, maintenance, mapping, bandwidth, operator workload, local responders, and victims can matter as much as the mobile platform. Surgical systems, assistive devices, teleoperation, and other augmentations extend human reach or capability, while social robots trigger empathy, anthropomorphism, uncanniness, and emergent workplace norms. The central design question becomes which partner contributes which capability and retains which responsibility.

Taken together, the six topics replace the image of a self-contained mechanical person with a more useful systems view. A robot is simultaneously a material structure, a feedback process, a product of historical imagination, an element of infrastructure, a unit of labor or force, and a participant in human relationships. Its autonomy is always bounded by goals, hardware, data, environments, ownership, and institutions. Good robotics therefore depends not only on making a machine work, but on making its limits, control, consequences, and human partnerships legible.
