# Elementary Algebra

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Elementary Algebra organizes the number systems, symbolic forms, operations, equations, and inequalities that generalize arithmetic. It is a chapter-tier topic, so Full Notes link to its focused child tags instead of directly to this page.

## Overview Chapter

Elementary algebra begins with a change in viewpoint. Arithmetic evaluates operations on particular numbers, while algebra uses symbols to express relationships that remain true across many possible values. A letter may stand for an arbitrary number, an unknown to be found, or a quantity that varies with a situation. This symbolic language makes it possible to state general properties, simplify complicated calculations, and translate practical relationships into equations or inequalities. The subject is cumulative: reliable symbolic work depends on the properties of numbers and operations, and later solution methods depend on understanding expressions.

### Numbers and the laws of arithmetic

[[Number Systems and Arithmetic]] establishes the objects and structural laws that algebra generalizes. Natural numbers support counting and ordering, integers add opposites so subtraction can remain within a number system, rational numbers add quotients of integers, and irrational numbers complete the real-number line beyond fractional representation. A number line connects order with position and makes absolute value interpretable as distance from zero.

Operations have properties that justify symbolic rearrangement. Closure asks whether an operation stays within a set. An identity element leaves a value unchanged. Commutativity permits a change in order, associativity permits a change in grouping, and distributivity connects multiplication with addition. These properties are not decorative labels: combining like terms, expanding products, factoring expressions, and preserving equality all depend on them. Knowing which operations lack a property is equally important; subtraction and division, for example, are not commutative.

### Fractions and decimal representation

[[Fractions and Decimal Forms]] extends division beyond the natural numbers. A fraction records a numerator divided by a nonzero denominator. Different numerator-denominator pairs can represent the same point on the number line, so the cancellation law explains equivalent fractions and reduction to lowest terms. Addition and subtraction require a common denominator, while multiplication acts directly on numerators and denominators. Division becomes multiplication by the reciprocal.

Decimal form connects fractions to positional notation. A rational number has a decimal expansion that terminates or repeats, and a repeating decimal can be converted back to a fraction by aligning and subtracting repeated tails. An irrational number instead has an infinite nonrepeating expansion. These representations show why exact equality must be distinguished from approximation: a short decimal can approximate an irrational quantity without becoming equal to it.

### Reading and simplifying expressions

[[Algebraic Expressions]] treats an expression as a structured combination of numbers, variables, operations, and grouping symbols. Its terms are separated by addition or subtraction, and numerical coefficients scale their variable parts. Like terms have identical variable factors and exponents, so the distributive property permits their coefficients to be combined. Unlike terms cannot be merged merely because they occur in the same expression.

Evaluation substitutes specified values for variables and then follows the grouping and operation structure. Simplification produces an equivalent form by suppressing parentheses correctly, distributing factors, and combining like terms. Translation works in the other direction: verbal relationships such as a sum, difference, product, quotient, square, or reciprocal become symbolic expressions. The important discipline is preserving order and grouping rather than translating one word at a time without regard to the relationship described.

### Exponents, powers, and inverse operations

[[Exponents and Roots]] gives concise notation for repeated multiplication and its reversal. In a power, the base is the repeated factor and the exponent records how many copies occur. The laws of exponents follow from this meaning: multiplying powers with a common base adds exponents, division subtracts them when the base is nonzero, a power raised to a power multiplies exponents, and a power of a product distributes the exponent across its factors.

Zero and negative exponents extend the pattern consistently. A nonzero base to the zero power equals one, while a negative exponent denotes the reciprocal of the corresponding positive power. Roots reverse exponentiation: a square root is a number whose square gives the radicand, and a cube root is a number whose cube does so. The notation is compact, but parentheses remain crucial because the square of a negative number differs from the negative of a square.

### Finding and expressing patterns

[[Sequences and Algebraic Patterns]] uses variables to replace a list of separate cases with a general term. A sequence has an ordered position, and a law of formation explains how its values are produced. An arithmetic progression changes by a constant difference, giving a linear formula for the term at position (n). A geometric progression changes by a constant ratio, giving an exponential formula.

Pattern recognition in algebra is not a guess detached from evidence. A proposed general term must reproduce the listed cases and explain the operation that advances from one position to the next. Pairing terms from opposite ends of an arithmetic sequence also exposes a constant pair sum, turning a long addition into a short product. These methods show algebra functioning as a language for regularity.

### Polynomials and rational expressions

[[Polynomial and Rational Expressions]] applies the laws of arithmetic to expressions built from powers and coefficients. Monomials contain one term, binomials two, and trinomials three. Polynomial multiplication distributes every term of one factor across every term of the other and then combines like terms. The FOIL mnemonic is a special case for two binomials, not a separate law.

Polynomial long division repeats a cycle: divide leading terms, multiply the divisor, subtract, and continue until the remainder has lower degree than the divisor. Rational expressions are quotients of algebraic expressions and are undefined wherever their denominator is zero. They are simplified by factoring and cancelling common factors, added through common denominators, and divided by multiplying by a reciprocal. Cancellation removes factors, not arbitrary terms joined by addition.

### Factoring and reusable identities

[[Factoring and Algebraic Identities]] reverses expansion by expressing a sum as a product. The first step is often to remove the greatest factor common to every term. When no factor spans the full expression, grouping can expose a repeated binomial factor. Quadratic trinomials can be factored by finding numbers whose product and sum match the required coefficients.

Special identities make recurring structures immediately recognizable. The square of a sum or difference produces two square terms and a doubled product. A difference of squares factors into conjugate binomials. Sums and differences of cubes use a linear factor paired with a quadratic factor. These identities support exact mental calculations, multi-stage factorization, and equation solving. A complete factorization may require applying more than one pattern in sequence.

### Linear equations and modeling

[[Linear Equations and Modeling]] distinguishes an equation that is true only for particular values from an algebraic identity true for all permissible values. Solving an equation means finding values that make both sides equal. Valid transformations preserve the balance: adding equal quantities to both sides or multiplying both sides by the same nonzero quantity produces an equivalent equation.

Linear equations isolate an unknown whose highest power is one. Fractions may be removed by multiplying through by a common denominator, and proportions may be solved through cross multiplication when denominators are nonzero. A proposed solution is verified by substitution into the original equation. For word problems, the central work occurs before manipulation: assign a variable, express related quantities consistently, build the equation, solve it, and interpret the result in context. Working backward can also undo a known sequence of operations.

### Quadratic equations

[[Quadratic Equations]] studies equations whose highest power of the unknown is two. Standard form places all terms on one side as (ax^2 + bx + c = 0), with (a\neq0). When the quadratic factors, the zero-product property converts one equation into separate linear equations: if a product is zero, at least one factor must be zero.

Completing the square turns (x^2 + bx) into a perfect-square trinomial by adding ((b/2)^2). The quadratic formula generalizes this method and gives solutions even when convenient integer factorization is unavailable. Some equations that initially contain fractions or higher-looking structures can be rearranged into quadratic form. Regardless of method, each candidate solution should be checked against the original equation and any restrictions introduced by denominators.

### Inequalities and intervals

[[Linear Inequalities and Intervals]] replaces equality with an ordering relation. Adding the same quantity to both sides preserves the relation, as does multiplying by a positive quantity. Multiplication or division by a negative quantity reverses the direction because it reflects positions across zero on the number line.

An inequality generally describes a solution set rather than a single value. Intervals provide a compact way to represent consecutive portions of the real line. Open endpoints exclude a boundary and closed endpoints include it; intervals may be bounded on both sides or extend without bound. Compound inequalities combine constraints through intersection or union. Graphing the result makes endpoint inclusion and direction visible, while a word problem determines whether phrases such as "at least," "no more than," or "between" require strict or inclusive boundaries.

Across these topics, elementary algebra is a discipline of preserving relationships while changing form. Arithmetic properties justify each rewrite, symbolic notation exposes general structure, and verification checks that a transformation still answers the original question. This foundation supports later mathematics because it makes abstraction operational: a relationship can be represented, manipulated, tested, and reused beyond the particular numbers that first revealed it.
