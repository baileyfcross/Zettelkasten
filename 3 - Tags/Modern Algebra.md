# Modern Algebra

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Modern Algebra is the chapter-level topic for algebraic structures, homomorphisms, quotient constructions, polynomial factorization, field extensions, Galois theory, and solvability. Full Notes should link to a focused child topic rather than directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Modern algebra studies structures by identifying operations and the laws they satisfy. Instead of treating arithmetic as a collection of isolated techniques, it asks which conclusions follow from associativity, identities, inverses, distributivity, commutativity, and closure. This viewpoint reveals the same structure in integers, modular systems, permutations, polynomial rings, fields, and geometric construction problems. It also creates a sequence of increasingly powerful quotient constructions: equivalence classes produce partitions, subgroup cosets produce quotient groups, ideals produce quotient rings, and irreducible polynomials produce extension fields.

[[Set Relations and Algebraic Foundations]] supplies the language on which every later definition rests. A [[Set]] provides the carrier of a structure, while subsets become substructures only after closure and the relevant axioms are verified. [[Equivalence Relation|Equivalence relations]] organize elements into disjoint [[Equivalence Class|equivalence classes]], and the resulting [[Partition of a Set|partitions]] explain why quotient elements can be represented by many underlying values. [[Mathematical Induction]] supports arguments about repeated operations and finite products, while Cartesian products provide the underlying sets for direct-product structures.

[[Functions and Elementary Number Theory]] introduces the maps and arithmetic used throughout the subject. Injective, surjective, and bijective functions distinguish embeddings, quotient maps, and isomorphisms. Composition later becomes the operation in automorphism groups. Divisibility, the [[Euclidean Algorithm]], and [[Bezout Identity|Bézout's identity]] connect greatest common divisors to modular inverses. These elementary results reappear in cyclic groups, polynomial division, relative primality, and irreducibility.

[[Group Operations and Structure]] begins with a [[Binary Operation]] and adds associativity, a [[Group Identity]], and an [[Inverse Element]] to obtain a [[Group]]. An [[Abelian Group]] adds commutativity, but permutation groups show why that property cannot be assumed. [[Element Order]] describes the cyclic behavior generated by one element, [[Modular Arithmetic]] supplies concrete finite examples, and [[Direct Product of Groups|direct products]] build larger groups coordinatewise. A [[Cayley Table]] makes a finite operation visible, although associativity still requires proof.

[[Subgroups and Group Homomorphisms]] studies the internal pieces and structure-preserving maps of a group. The [[Subgroup Test]] packages closure and inverses into a usable criterion. A [[Cyclic Group]] is generated by one element and is necessarily abelian. A [[Group Homomorphism]] preserves multiplication, with the [[Kernel of Group Homomorphism|kernel]] recording what collapses and the [[Image of Group Homomorphism|image]] recording what is reached. An injective and surjective homomorphism is a [[Group Isomorphism]], showing that two representations have the same group structure. [[Permutation Group|Permutation groups]] and the [[Symmetric Group]] make these ideas concrete through function composition.

[[Quotient Groups and Finite Group Theory]] turns coset partitions into new groups. [[Left Coset|Left]] and [[Right Coset|right cosets]] have equal size and partition the group, yielding [[Lagrange Theorem|Lagrange's theorem]] and the [[Index of a Subgroup]]. A [[Normal Subgroup]] is exactly what makes coset multiplication independent of representatives, producing a [[Quotient Group]]. The [[First Isomorphism Theorem for Groups]] identifies a homomorphic image with the source modulo its kernel. [[Cauchy Theorem|Cauchy's theorem]] and the [[Sylow Theorem|Sylow theorems]] then use prime divisors of group order to force and constrain finite subgroups.

[[Ring and Ideal Foundations]] adds multiplication to an abelian additive group and connects the two operations by distributivity. Ring identities, units, zero divisors, subrings, homomorphisms, and polynomial rings show which familiar arithmetic properties survive. The topic is shared with [[Commutative Algebra]] because modern algebra introduces the elementary structures that commutative algebra develops further.

[[Quotient Rings and Ideals]] explains why an additive subgroup is not enough for a ring quotient. An [[Ideal]] absorbs multiplication by arbitrary ring elements, making multiplication of cosets well defined. The [[Quotient Map for Rings]] has the chosen ideal as its kernel, and the [[First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings]] identifies every homomorphic image with such a quotient. [[Prime Ideal|Prime]] and [[Maximal Ideal|maximal ideals]] are characterized by quotients that are integral domains and fields, respectively.

[[Integral Domains and Ring Characteristic]] studies how close a commutative ring is to ordinary number arithmetic. Removing [[Zero Divisor|zero divisors]] yields an [[Integral Domain]] and restores cancellation. Every [[Finite Integral Domain]] is a [[Field]]. The [[Characteristic of a Ring]] measures the additive order of its identity; a domain has characteristic zero or prime characteristic. An [[Ordered Integral Domain]] adds an order compatible with addition and positive multiplication and therefore must have characteristic zero.

[[Polynomial Rings and Factorization]] treats polynomials as formal ring elements rather than merely as functions. Degree controls multiplication, root counts, and division. Over a field, the [[Polynomial Division Algorithm]] leads to the [[Factor Theorem]], while the [[Rational Root Theorem]] and [[Eisenstein Criterion]] provide irreducibility tests over the rationals. [[Unique Factorization of Polynomials]] expresses every nonconstant polynomial as irreducible factors, up to order and nonzero constants.

[[Field Extensions and Polynomial Roots]] enlarges a field so that missing roots can exist. An [[Algebraic Element]] satisfies a polynomial over the base field, while a [[Transcendental Element]] does not. [[Field Adjunction]] forms the smallest field K(c), and the [[Minimal Polynomial]] determines both its quotient construction and its finite basis. The [[Degree of Field Extension]] measures that basis, and a [[Root Field]] contains all roots needed to split a polynomial.

Finally, [[Galois Theory and Solvability]] makes field symmetry into group theory. A [[Field Automorphism]] fixing the base permutes roots, and these maps form the [[Galois Group]]. The [[Fixed Field]] construction reverses subgroup inclusion, producing the [[Galois Correspondence]] between intermediate fields and subgroups. Towers of quadratic extensions characterize straightedge-and-compass [[Constructible Number|constructible numbers]], explaining the impossibility of general [[Angle Trisection]], [[Doubling the Cube]], and [[Squaring the Circle]]. More broadly, a polynomial is [[Solvable by Radicals]] exactly when its Galois group is a [[Solvable Group]]. The failure of the symmetric groups from degree five onward therefore explains why no general radical formula exists for every higher-degree polynomial.
