# Applied Linear Algebra and Optimization

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Applied Linear Algebra and Optimization is the chapter-level topic for representing quantitative systems with matrices, solving them reliably, approximating data, and selecting the best feasible decisions. ## Overview Chapter

Applied linear algebra begins by representing many related quantities as one structured object. [[Matrix and Vector Computation]] defines the basic operations: vectors organize coordinates or unknowns, matrices arrange coefficients and transformations, and products combine many weighted relationships at once. [[Matrix Structure]] then distinguishes forms such as square, rectangular, diagonal, triangular, symmetric, and skew-symmetric matrices. Those forms are not cosmetic labels. They determine whether a determinant or inverse is defined, how much data must be stored, and which algorithms can exploit zeros, symmetry, or diagonal entries.

[[Matrix Invertibility and Factorization]] connects structural properties to efficient computation. A nonsingular matrix has full rank, a nonzero determinant, and a unique inverse. Factorizations avoid repeating the entire elimination process for every right-hand side. LU decomposition separates a matrix into lower and upper triangular factors; Doolittle and Crout choose different unit diagonals; Cholesky uses symmetry and positive definiteness to write a matrix as a triangular factor times its transpose; and LDLT separates the diagonal scaling. Permutation matrices record row exchanges, while banded and tridiagonal structure reduce both storage and arithmetic.

The equations being solved belong to [[Linear System Structure]]. A simultaneous linear system becomes the compact matrix equation $Ax=b$, and its augmented matrix records both coefficients and constants. Homogeneous systems always admit the zero solution, while nonhomogeneous systems may have one, none, or infinitely many solutions. The relationship between the coefficient rank, augmented rank, and number of unknowns distinguishes consistent, inconsistent, underdetermined, and overdetermined cases.

Two families of algorithms act on that structure. [[Direct Linear System Solvers]] use a finite chain of row operations or factorizations. Gaussian elimination produces an upper triangular system for back substitution, Gauss-Jordan elimination continues to reduced row echelon form, and Cramer's rule expresses components through determinants. Pivoting improves numerical behavior by replacing a weak pivot with a more suitable entry. [[Iterative Linear System Methods]] instead start from an approximation and repeatedly improve it. Jacobi updates components from the preceding iterate; Gauss-Seidel uses fresh components immediately; successive over-relaxation changes the strength of the update; conjugate gradient searches along mutually conjugate directions for a symmetric positive-definite system; and iterative refinement solves residual equations to repair an approximate answer.

[[Eigenvalue Theory]] studies directions that a matrix transformation only scales. The equation $Ax=\lambda x$ leads to the characteristic polynomial and to an eigenspace for each eigenvalue. Similar matrices describe the same transformation in different bases and preserve eigenvalues. When enough independent eigenvectors exist, diagonalization replaces a coupled transformation with independent scalar actions, making powers, recurrences, and systems of differential equations easier to analyze.

For large matrices, the characteristic polynomial is an impractical computational route, so [[Numerical Eigenvalue Methods]] use iteration and orthogonal transformations. Power and inverse-power ideas target extreme or nearby eigenvalues. Gershgorin disks bound their locations, and the Rayleigh quotient estimates an eigenvalue from an approximate eigenvector. Jacobi rotations, Givens rotations, and Householder reflections progressively eliminate entries without changing the spectrum. The QR algorithm builds a sequence of similar matrices that approaches triangular form, while singular value decomposition extends related structure to rectangular matrices and exposes rank and conditioning.

When a function is known only through tabulated values, [[Interpolation Methods]] constructs an approximating polynomial. Lagrange basis polynomials provide a direct expression, Newton divided differences make the polynomial easy to extend, Aitken's recursion evaluates the interpolant progressively, and Chebyshev nodes control the damaging endpoint oscillation of high-degree fits. Interpolation requires exact agreement at the given points; it is most appropriate when those values are treated as exact.

Noisy or incompatible data instead motivates [[Least Squares Methods]]. A least squares model minimizes the sum of squared residuals rather than passing through every observation. Lines, polynomials, planes, and nonlinear models all fit this pattern. For $Ax\approx b$, the normal equation makes the residual orthogonal to the column space. QR decomposition avoids explicitly squaring the condition number through $A^TA$, singular value decomposition provides a stable view of rank, and the pseudoinverse unifies overdetermined approximations with minimum-norm solutions of underdetermined systems.

Optimization asks which candidate should be chosen. [[Optimization and Differentiation]] supplies objective functions, gradients, Hessians, Taylor approximations, stationary points, convexity, and curvature tests. These concepts explain how local change indicates ascent or descent and when a local extremum is trustworthy. [[Linear Programming Methods]] specializes the objective and constraints to linear expressions. Slack, surplus, and artificial variables produce standard form, the simplex method moves among basic feasible solutions, duality provides bounds and economic interpretations, and sensitivity analysis tracks how an optimum responds to changed model data.

[[Nonlinear Optimization Methods]] handles objectives or constraints containing nonlinear interactions. Bisection, fixed-point iteration, and Newton's method solve nonlinear equations. Golden-section search and quadratic interpolation find one-dimensional extrema, while steepest ascent and descent use gradients in several dimensions. Equality constraints introduce Lagrange multipliers, inequalities lead to Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, and reduced-gradient, separable-programming, quadratic-programming, and Wolfe procedures exploit additional structure.

Every computed answer must be interpreted through [[Numerical Error and Conditioning]]. Floating-point numbers have finite mantissas and exponent ranges, so rounding and chopping are unavoidable. Truncation introduces a different error by stopping an infinite or iterative process. Absolute and relative error describe the discrepancy, vector and matrix norms measure its size, and a large condition number warns that small input perturbations can create large solution changes. A stable algorithm limits avoidable amplification but cannot remove sensitivity inherent in the mathematical problem.

The abstractions underneath these methods are organized in [[Vector Space Geometry]]. Linear combinations, span, independence, bases, dimension, and subspaces explain rank, null spaces, coordinate changes, and eigenspaces. Inner products add length and orthogonality, producing orthonormal bases and orthogonal matrices. [[Complex Linear Algebra]] extends this geometry when entries and eigenvalues are complex: conjugation replaces ordinary transposition in inner products and Hermitian matrices generalize real symmetric matrices.

Finally, [[MATLAB Numerical Computing]] turns the mathematics into an executable workflow. Its command window and workspace support interactive exploration, while matrix syntax, colon-based indexing, element-wise operators, plotting, and function files express reusable algorithms. The software does not replace the mathematics: selecting matrix multiplication instead of element-wise multiplication, checking dimensions, inspecting conditioning, and choosing an appropriate solver all depend on understanding the structures represented in the code.

Together, these topics form a continuous chain. Vector-space structure gives matrices meaning; matrix structure determines solving strategies; eigenvalues and factorizations reveal transformations; interpolation and least squares reconstruct functions from data; optimization selects among alternatives; and error analysis establishes how much confidence a numerical result deserves.
