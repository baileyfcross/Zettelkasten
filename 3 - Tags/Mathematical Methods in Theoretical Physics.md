# Mathematical Methods in Theoretical Physics

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Mathematical methods in theoretical physics connect symbolic structure, field equations, transformations, approximation, and computational checking to the models used across mechanics, electromagnetism, thermodynamics, quantum theory, and relativity.

## Overview Chapter

The mathematical language of physics begins with representations that preserve magnitude, direction, phase, and coordinate dependence. [[Plane Trigonometry]] supplies angular relations, periodic identities, and the radian measure used in rotations and waves. [[Complex Linear Algebra]] adds magnitude and phase through complex numbers, conjugation, Hermitian structure, and polar form. [[Matrix and Vector Computation]] collects components into objects that can express simultaneous equations, rotations, linear operators, and eigenvalue problems. [[Differential Calculus]] then turns a function into its local rate of change, providing the rules needed to translate physical laws into differential equations.

[[Vector Algebra and Geometry]] connects component calculation to magnitude, direction, and spatial interpretation. Vector addition composes displacements, scalar multiplication changes scale and orientation, and linear combinations reveal whether a set contains redundant directions. Dot and cross products encode projection, angle, area, and perpendicular direction, while scalar and vector triple products extend those relationships to volume and nested three-dimensional operations. A basis turns the geometric object into components without confusing the coordinates with the vector itself.

[[Multivariable Differential Calculus]] extends local change from a curve to a surface or field. Partial derivatives hold all but one coordinate fixed, directional derivatives combine those rates along a chosen direction, and mixed derivatives describe how one rate changes with another variable. The total differential collects first-order changes, exact differentials identify expressions that come from one scalar function, and multivariable Taylor expansions provide local polynomial models. Stationary points are found where all first derivatives vanish and classified from second-order behavior.

Accumulation and approximation extend that local view. [[Integral Calculus and Series]] covers substitutions, integration by parts, multiple and improper integrals, Gaussian integrals, Taylor expansions, convergence, and the gamma function. An integral combines infinitesimal contributions over a path, area, volume, or unbounded domain; a convergent series replaces a difficult function with a controlled sum. Both require attention to their domains and limiting operations, because a symbolic antiderivative or formal series is not automatically valid everywhere.

Spatially distributed quantities are organized by [[Vector Calculus and Integral Theorems]]. A scalar field assigns one value to each point, while a vector field assigns magnitude and direction. The gradient converts scalar variation into a vector, divergence measures local source or sink behavior, curl measures circulation, and the Laplacian combines second derivatives. Flux and line integrals describe fields over surfaces and paths. The divergence and Stokes theorems then equate volume or surface derivatives with boundary integrals, allowing a local field law and a global conservation statement to be treated as two forms of the same relationship.

Evolution problems lead to [[Ordinary Differential Equations and Phase Space]]. The order, degree, linearity, and homogeneity of an equation identify which solution methods are available. Separation and integrating factors solve important first-order cases; characteristic roots handle linear constant-coefficient equations; Wronskians test whether solution functions are independent; and power series extend solutions around ordinary points. Coupled equations replace one trajectory with motion through a higher-dimensional state space, where a phase-space curve displays how the complete state changes rather than only one coordinate over time.

When a closed form is unavailable, [[Numerical ODE Methods]] advances an approximation from supplied initial or boundary data. Picard and Taylor procedures construct local series; Euler and Runge-Kutta methods step forward from one computed state; and Milne or Adams-Bashforth formulas reuse several earlier states in predictor-corrector calculations. Convergence asks whether refinement approaches the exact solution, stability asks whether perturbations remain controlled, and finite-difference or shooting methods convert two-point boundary conditions into computable algebraic or initial-value problems.

Discrete-time models are organized by [[Difference Equation Methods]]. A difference equation relates shifted values of a sequence in the way a differential equation relates derivatives of a continuous function. Its order and degree classify the recurrence, while a shift-operator polynomial supplies an auxiliary equation for the complementary function. A particular integral accounts for forcing, initial values determine the arbitrary constants, and simultaneous recurrences represent several coupled sequences.

Many boundary-value problems produce recurring families of solutions. [[Special Functions and Orthogonal Expansions]] treats the Dirac delta and Heaviside step as generalized tools for localized sources and switching, then develops orthogonal bases whose coefficients isolate independent modes. Legendre, Laguerre, Hermite, Bessel, and spherical-harmonic functions arise because different differential equations and coordinate symmetries select different bases. Orthogonality extracts expansion coefficients, while completeness states that the basis can reconstruct the functions allowed by the problem.

[[Fourier Series and Integral Transforms]] changes the representation of a problem. A periodic function becomes a discrete sum of sine, cosine, or complex-exponential modes. A nonperiodic function uses a continuous Fourier transform, and its power spectrum describes how strength is distributed across frequency or spatial wave number. The Laplace transform adds exponential weighting and turns many initial-value differential equations into algebraic equations in the transform variable. In every case, the inverse transform is part of the method: a transformed expression is useful because it can be interpreted back in the original variables.

Distributed evolution is governed by [[Partial Differential Equations and Separation Methods]]. First-order PDEs include conservation and field equations; second-order PDEs are classified as elliptic, parabolic, or hyperbolic according to their derivative structure. Laplace and Poisson equations describe equilibrium fields, the heat equation describes diffusion, the wave equation describes propagation, and the Helmholtz equation describes spatial modes. Separation of variables assumes a product of single-coordinate functions, converts one PDE into several ODEs, and uses boundary or initial conditions to select the allowed separation constants and modes.

[[Numerical PDE Methods]] replaces derivatives and boundary conditions by relations among mesh values. Five-point formulas and relaxation solve elliptic Laplace or Poisson problems. Schmidt, Crank-Nicolson, Richardson, and Du Fort-Frankel schemes advance parabolic heat equations with different accuracy and stability behavior. Alternating-direction ideas extend the grid to two spatial dimensions, while a centered recurrence advances hyperbolic wave equations only when the time and space steps satisfy an appropriate stability restriction.

[[Complex Analysis and Conformal Mapping]] gains additional power from complex differentiability. The Cauchy-Riemann equations enforce the consistency required for a complex derivative to be independent of approach direction. Cauchy's theorem and integral formula relate values inside a region to a contour around it. Laurent series classify isolated singularities, and residues convert many contour integrals into algebraic sums. Conformal mappings preserve local angles and can carry a difficult boundary geometry into a simpler domain where harmonic solutions are easier to obtain.

The same methods organize physical motion in [[Analytical Mechanics and Chaotic Dynamics]]. A Lagrangian expresses dynamics through generalized coordinates and a stationary action principle; the Euler-Lagrange equations produce the equations of motion, including constrained systems. A Legendre transformation replaces velocities with generalized momenta and yields Hamilton's first-order equations and Poisson-bracket evolution. Nonlinear systems may be studied through fixed points, attractors, Lyapunov exponents, Poincare sections, and fractal dimension. Deterministic equations can remain difficult to predict because nearby initial states separate exponentially.

Field theory gives these tools a concrete synthesis in [[Electromagnetic Field Foundations]]. Scalar and vector potentials, Gauss and Ampere laws, Maxwell's equations, constitutive relations, and the Poynting vector connect charge, current, matter, and energy flow. Electrostatic and magnetostatic problems often reduce to Laplace or Poisson equations, while time-dependent fields yield wave equations and radiation. Coordinate symmetry, multipole expansions, and harmonic representations determine which mathematical basis makes a problem tractable.

At microscopic scales, [[Quantum Physics and Cosmology]] uses complex wavefunctions, operators, expectation values, normalization, uncertainty, and boundary conditions. The Schrodinger equation is an eigenvalue problem for stationary states and an evolution equation for time-dependent states. Quantized energies follow when only selected solutions satisfy the boundaries. Orthogonal polynomials and spherical harmonics describe oscillator and atomic states, while approximation methods estimate systems whose exact equations cannot be solved.

[[Thermal and Statistical Physics]] connects macroscopic variables to weighted collections of microstates. Entropy counts compatible arrangements, and an ensemble states which quantities are fixed or exchanged. The partition function normalizes Boltzmann weights and generates average energy, free energy, entropy, and heat capacity through derivatives. Canonical, grand-canonical, Bose-Einstein, and Fermi-Dirac descriptions differ in which states and particle numbers are permitted, but all turn microscopic energy spectra into observable thermodynamic behavior.

Spacetime geometry belongs to [[Gravitation and Relativity]]. Lorentz transformations preserve the spacetime interval and lead to time dilation, length contraction, velocity addition, and the relativistic energy-momentum relation. General relativity promotes the metric to a position-dependent description of geometry. Covariant derivatives correct ordinary differentiation for changing coordinate bases; geodesics describe free motion; curvature tensors measure the failure of flat-space relationships; and Einstein's field equation relates that curvature to matter and energy.

Finally, [[Relativistic Quantum Mechanics]] combines relativistic energy-momentum with quantum wave equations. The Klein-Gordon equation supplies a second-order relativistic field equation but does not give a positive-definite single-particle probability density. The first-order Dirac equation introduces spinor components and gamma matrices, produces a conserved positive density, and admits positive- and negative-energy solutions. Its nonrelativistic limit returns the familiar low-energy theory, demonstrating how a broader mathematical framework contains an earlier approximation.

Computation supports every stage of this work, but it does not replace analysis. Symbolic or numerical software can expand expressions, solve equations, plot fields, and test candidate solutions; the physicist still has to select coordinates, impose valid conditions, track dimensions, and decide whether a result belongs to the original problem. Mathematical methods are therefore best understood as transformations that preserve meaning while making structure visible.
