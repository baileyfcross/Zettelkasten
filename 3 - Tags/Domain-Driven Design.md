# Domain-Driven Design

Parent topic: [[Software Design Patterns and Application Architecture]]

Domain-Driven Design organizes software around a shared understanding of a business domain, explicit model boundaries, and behavior-rich models that preserve business rules.

## Overview Chapter

Domain-Driven Design begins before code. [[Domain Discovery and Ubiquitous Language]] separates the problem a business is trying to solve from the technical solution chosen to solve it. The problem space contains current and desired states, constraints, and business rules; the solution space contains the software structures and steps proposed in response. [[Essential Complexity]] belongs to the problem itself, while [[Accidental Complexity]] is introduced by frameworks, databases, integration choices, and other solution mechanisms. This distinction keeps architectural machinery from being mistaken for business value. Teams reduce uncertainty through [[Domain Knowledge]] and [[Knowledge Crunching]], gathering information from multiple experts and testing their assumptions. The resulting [[Ubiquitous Language]] must be used consistently in conversation, code, tests, interfaces, and data within one context. When a term changes meaning, the team has found evidence of a contextual boundary rather than a reason to force one universal definition.

[[EventStorming Collaborative Modeling]] turns that learning into a visible, participatory model. Participants first record [[Domain Event]]s: past-tense facts that changed the state of the business. Arranging them on an [[Event Timeline]] reveals order, concurrency, missing steps, and interactions among systems. The large modeling surface lets many people work at once instead of routing every idea through a single diagrammer. Domain experts contribute how work actually happens, while developers and other participants ask questions that expose assumptions. An [[EventStorming Hotspot]] marks disagreement or missing knowledge so the workshop can continue without pretending the uncertainty has been resolved. External systems are shown explicitly because events can cross organizational or technical control boundaries. The model is temporary by design, but its discoveries guide vocabulary, scope, prototypes, and follow-up investigation.

The discoveries become software through [[Domain Model Building Blocks]]. A [[Domain Model]] represents a proposed solution using the concepts and behavior of the domain; it is not merely a database schema. An [[Anemic Domain Model]] separates data from the rules that operate on it, making behavior difficult to locate and understand. A [[Domain Entity]] is tracked by identity through change, whereas a [[Value Object]] is defined by its values and can validate a domain-specific type before it enters an entity. A [[Domain Object Factory]] creates valid domain objects when construction requires named rules or conversion. A [[Domain Service]] expresses domain behavior that does not naturally belong to one entity while keeping infrastructure implementations outside the model. [[Entity Invariant]]s prevent behavior from leaving an entity in an invalid state, and event-applied transitions keep the fact of change coupled to the state it produces.

[[Application Commands and Service Boundaries]] connect that model to the outside world without allowing delivery and infrastructure concerns to take it over. [[Domain Model Isolation]] keeps persistence, transport, serialization, and user-interface mechanisms outside the core. A [[Public API Contract]] describes serialized requests with stable boundary types such as a [[Data Transfer Object]]. An [[Application Command]] expresses an actor's intent to change state, while a [[Command Handler]] or [[Application Service]] loads the relevant model, invokes its behavior, and persists the result. HTTP is one possible delivery mechanism, not part of the domain itself. This separation allows the model to be tested in memory and lets transport or persistence choices evolve without rewriting the business rules.

[[Aggregate Consistency and Persistence]] defines where atomic business correctness is required. An [[Aggregate]] is a small group of entities and value objects that changes as one consistency unit. Its [[Aggregate Root]] is the only entry point exposed to callers and protects rules spanning the aggregate. The aggregate boundary is also a [[Transaction Boundary]]: an operation either leaves the whole aggregate valid or leaves it unchanged. Other aggregates are referenced by identity rather than held in one enormous object graph. [[Optimistic Concurrency]] detects whether another command changed an aggregate after it was read. The [[Repository Pattern]] abstracts loading and saving aggregate state, and a [[Unit of Work]] coordinates the persistence commit. These abstractions do not remove [[Persistence Impedance Mismatch]]; document serializers and relational mappings can still pressure a domain model to adopt storage-specific shapes, so those compromises must remain visible.

Writes and reads often need different shapes. [[CQRS Query and Read Model Design]] separates state-changing commands from information-returning queries. [[Command Query Responsibility Segregation]] can begin within one application and one database; it does not require separate services or storage. A repository serves aggregate behavior on the command side, but an [[Application Query]] can retrieve a purpose-built [[Read Model]] directly. This avoids forcing cross-aggregate display needs through a single aggregate repository. Read models should use the same domain language as the user decision they support and may combine data from several aggregates. The [[Read-to-Write Mismatch]] explains why indexing and normalization choices that favor writes can make reads awkward, and vice versa. CQRS accepts that asymmetry rather than stretching one representation across incompatible jobs.

[[Event Sourcing and Projections]] changes persistence from storing the latest snapshot to storing the facts that produced it. In [[Event Sourcing]], new domain events are appended to an [[Event Stream]], normally one stream per aggregate. An [[Event Store]] preserves their order and lets the application rebuild an [[Event-Sourced Aggregate]] by folding those events through its transition function. This [[Aggregate Rehydration]] makes history the source of truth and supports auditing and replay, but it also creates new operational responsibilities. A [[Projection]] consumes events to build query-oriented models. A [[Catch-Up Subscription]] processes history before switching to live events, while a [[Projection Checkpoint]] records the last successfully processed position. The separation makes read models rebuildable but commonly introduces [[Eventual Consistency]] between a committed event and the projections that have not yet processed it.

Finally, [[Bounded Context and Organizational Design]] prevents a complex organization from collapsing into one ambiguous model. A [[Bounded Context]] establishes the boundary within which a model and its language are internally consistent. The [[Single Model Trap]] appears when one representation is forced to serve unrelated business capabilities, usually accumulating conflicting meanings and rates of change. Left unchecked, that produces a [[Big Ball of Mud]]. [[Linguistic Boundary]] analysis finds places where the same word carries different meanings; a [[Context Map]] then records contexts and their relationships. Boundaries also shape work. [[Conway's Law]] predicts that architecture will reflect communication structures, so business-aligned teams need clear [[Bounded Context Ownership]]. [[Work in Progress Limit]]s reduce queues and context switching, while [[Loose Coupling and High Alignment]] lets teams coordinate on outcomes without sharing every internal implementation decision.

Together, these practices keep software aligned with what people are trying to accomplish. The strategic practices discover language and boundaries; the tactical practices encode behavior and consistency; CQRS and event sourcing provide optional structures for asymmetric or historical data; and organizational ownership keeps those boundaries workable over time. None of the patterns is valuable merely because it has a name. Each earns its place by reducing ambiguity, protecting a real rule, or allowing a team to change one part of the system without destabilizing the rest.
