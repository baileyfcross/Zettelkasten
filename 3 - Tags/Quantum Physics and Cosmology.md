# Quantum Physics and Cosmology

Parent topics: [[Physics]] · [[Mathematical Methods in Theoretical Physics]]

Quantum physics and cosmology connect experimentally grounded quantum principles to computational models of complex quantum systems, including adaptive circuits, quantum learning architectures, and simulations of high-energy phenomena.

## Overview Chapter

Quantum physics begins by replacing the continuous, deterministic expectations of classical mechanics with states, amplitudes, quantized outcomes, and probability. Cosmology extends physical reasoning to the history and large-scale structure of the universe. Although their characteristic scales are very different, both fields depend on a common discipline: infer hidden structure from measurements, represent that structure mathematically, and distinguish what a model predicts from what an experiment can actually resolve. Modern quantum computing adds a third layer. It uses controlled quantum systems not merely as objects of study but as processors that can represent, transform, and measure other quantum models.

The starting point is [[Foundational Quantum Physics and Cosmology]]. Blackbody spectra and the photoelectric effect motivate discrete energy exchange, while matter-wave interference and the Schrodinger equation replace classical trajectories with evolving probability amplitudes. Quantized bound states, uncertainty relations, and boundary conditions show that quantum behavior follows from the structure of admissible states rather than from imperfect instruments. On larger scales, redshift-distance measurements and relic radiation support an expanding universe that evolved from a hot, dense state. These ideas establish the evidential and mathematical background needed before a quantum circuit can be interpreted as a physical model rather than only as a formal computation.

[[Superconducting Qubit Engineering]] turns that model into a controllable physical machine. Josephson nonlinearity creates an addressable artificial atom, while transmon, split-transmon, and fluxonium circuits trade anharmonicity, tunability, and noise susceptibility in different ways. Capacitive and inductive interactions support exchange and conditional-phase gates; microwave phase control, DRAG shaping, and dynamical decoupling manage leakage and dephasing. Dispersive resonators convert qubit state into a microwave response, with Purcell filters and quantum-limited parametric amplifiers balancing measurement speed, lifetime, and signal-to-noise ratio. The result is a hardware stack in which circuit topology, environmental spectra, pulse design, and readout cannot be optimized independently.

Quantum algorithms turn that background into an engineering problem. A parameterized circuit represents a family of states or transformations, and a classical procedure selects parameters from measured outcomes. [[Variable-Structure Variational Quantum Circuits]] expands this idea by optimizing the circuit's gate structure as well as its numerical angles. Adding or removing identity-compiling blocks lets the model search for a compact architecture instead of accepting a fixed ansatz whose depth may be poorly matched to the device. This matters because every unnecessary gate increases exposure to noise, while an underspecified circuit may fail to represent the desired state.

[[Symmetry-Equivariant Variational Quantum Learning]] supplies a complementary way to constrain circuit design before optimization begins. A data embedding first turns a known transformation of the input into a unitary action on the Hilbert space. Group-twirled generators, invariant initial states, and invariant observables then produce predictions that cannot distinguish symmetry-related samples. The same gate construction can keep a ground-state search inside a conserved sector. This structural bias reduces parameters and can improve generalization or gradients, but it also creates a depth-dependent expressivity tradeoff: generators may vanish, the surviving gates may cease to be universal within the sector, and hardware compilation can turn an abstractly symmetric interaction into a long nonlocal circuit.

[[Error-Mitigation Trainability of Variational Quantum Algorithms]] asks whether correcting noisy expectation values also restores an optimizer's ability to navigate a cost landscape. Local noise can compress parameter-dependent signals faster than mitigation restores them, so zero-noise extrapolation or virtual distillation may reduce bias while increasing the shots needed to distinguish candidate parameters. Probabilistic cancellation can offer a small gain under global noise but accumulates overhead across local noisy gates. Linear regression methods occupy a narrower useful regime: they can leave pairwise resolution unchanged while repairing shifts or distortions in a still-resolvable landscape. The comparison makes finite-sample trainability, rather than infinite-sample accuracy alone, the relevant standard for near-term algorithms.

[[Scalable Error-Mitigated Quantum Simulation]] moves from variational trainability to the resource limits of large observable-estimation circuits. Sparse Pauli-Lindblad models turn circuit noise into a learnable inverse problem, while PEC, ZNE, and tensor-network mitigation expose different sampling exponents and systematic biases. TEM reaches the sampling lower bound by propagating inverse noise onto the observable, but its classical bond dimension creates a compression threshold and a distance-three-like residual error. Forecasts near one hundred qubits and one hundred layers remain conditional on low error rates, stable calibration, contraction resources, and a fair comparison with universal classical simulation rather than proof of achieved advantage.

[[Policy-Gradient Variational Quantum Compilation]] treats approximate circuit synthesis as sequential decision-making. A stochastic policy samples parameter updates, receives a reward based on endpoint fidelity, and learns which moves reach a target unitary with a shallow circuit. This nonlocal search can cross flat or misleading regions that defeat a purely local optimizer. The method illustrates a recurring hybrid pattern: the quantum device evaluates a physically meaningful quantity, while a classical learning loop organizes the search.

[[Meta-Learned Variational Quantum Optimization]] learns that classical search loop from a distribution of related quantum problems. A specialist LSTM observes only proposed circuit parameters and noisy expectation values at inference time, uses ten queries to locate a promising basin, and hands that point to Nelder-Mead for precision refinement. Training on small simulated QAOA or VQE instances can transfer to larger systems when the ansatz keeps the same parameter dimension, but the result is not a universal optimizer: meta-training can require up to a million circuit simulations, the reported noise model omits gate errors, and transfer across problem classes or changing parameter counts remains unresolved.

[[Natural-Gradient Optimization of Optical Quantum Circuits]] instead changes the local geometry used for every gradient step. The Fubini-Study metric measures distinguishable changes of the prepared quantum state rather than equal coordinate changes, and Wirtinger calculus extends that metric to optical gates with mixed real and complex parameters. Layerwise blocks, regularized pseudoinverses, and fidelity-based objectives make the update practical in small single-mode simulations. Single-photon and Hex-GKP preparation converge more smoothly and in fewer steps than tuned gradient descent or Adam, although the evidence covers only two noiseless simulated tasks and does not establish the cost or robustness of large multimode hardware.

Quantum learning architectures use the same state-and-measurement framework for inference. [[Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks]] arrange local unitaries and measurement-conditioned pooling into a hierarchy. Their structure can recognize phases of matter, learn error-correcting procedures, or transform a simple final measurement into an effective many-body observable. The architectural bias is valuable because unrestricted circuits are difficult to train and simulate; locality, parameter sharing, and progressive reduction constrain the model toward physically structured functions.

[[Quantum-Deformed Probabilistic Neural Networks]] explore a different connection between quantum dynamics and learning. They promote binary activations and weights into correlated quantum degrees of freedom before returning a thresholded classical result. The construction can represent dependencies unavailable to an independently sampled probabilistic layer, but practical study often requires low-entanglement restrictions so the model remains classically tractable. The comparison separates a quantum-inspired change in model structure from a demonstrated computational advantage.

[[Quantum Natural Language Processing for Financial Sentiment]] brings linguistic structure into this architectural picture. Quantum-enhanced recurrent cells replace the subnetworks inside an LSTM with variational circuits, whereas DisCoCat converts parsed grammatical composition into circuit connectivity. A financial-sentiment case study shows why these formal constructions must be judged together with their software: circuit simulation, postselection, parser availability, synthetic data, and unequal training budgets can dominate the apparent model comparison long before a quantum advantage is established.

[[Quantum Signal Processing for Derivative Pricing]] applies polynomial amplitude transformations to a fault-tolerant financial workload. Instead of computing exponentials and payoffs as reversible binary numbers before encoding them into a measurement probability, QSP composes those operations directly in amplitude space. Comparator-based encodings and Chebyshev approximations sharply lower estimated logical-qubit and T-gate requirements for an autocallable benchmark, but the resulting threshold still assumes thousands of logical qubits, fast logical non-Clifford gates, and a target function reducible to one transformed variable.

[[Quantum Risk Analysis of Derivative Portfolios]] lifts those pricing oracles into a higher-level market-risk computation. A scenario register coherently selects future market states, while QAE or QSP marks the lower tail needed for value at risk and conditional value at risk. Direct QSP thresholds avoid a costly digital price register and performed better in the reported finite-resource simulation, but the aggregate advantage depends on preparing a realistic high-dimensional scenario distribution cheaply enough that data loading does not consume the gain.

[[Quantum Chebyshev Modeling]] uses the same polynomial family for a different purpose: it makes Chebyshev values the amplitudes of an orthonormal feature basis. A dedicated transform connects that latent basis to computational bitstrings, letting one circuit family support pointwise learning, input differentiation, and projective sampling. The construction favors polynomial structure over Fourier periodicity and can extend a trained model onto a denser output register, while its ancilla handling, variational trainability, nonuniform node grid, and proposed multivariable scaling remain part of the practical cost.

[[Variational Quantum Wasserstein Anomaly Detection]] places a parameterized circuit inside the generator of a Wasserstein AnoGAN while leaving the critic and high-dimensional data processing classical. Latent rotations are converted into commuting Pauli-$Z$ expectations and then upscaled into transaction features, avoiding direct quantum loading of the observed records. On a credit-card fraud benchmark the hybrid model matches the final F1 score of simple classical generators but usually converges more slowly, and finite-shot simulation delays training further. Ansatz, initialization, latent-dimension, and shot-count ablations show how to test the quantum component without mistaking parity on an easy dataset for quantum advantage.

[[Experimental Quantum GAN Image Generation]] explores a complementary allocation of limited hardware. Patch qGANs concatenate probability vectors from several small circuit generators to produce larger images, while batch qGANs reserve index qubits for a proposed coherent minibatch calculation. A superconducting experiment generates recognizable eight-by-eight handwritten digits and controlled two-by-two gray-scale bars, but the quantitative parameter-efficiency evidence comes from the tiny synthetic task and the batch experiment uses batch size one. Ancilla postselection, parameter-shift measurements, state-preparation cost, and conditional hardware-noise evidence therefore remain part of the claimed resource advantage.

[[Quantum Generative Model Advantage Benchmarking]] makes the standard for an advantage claim explicit before comparing architectures. It separates provable, robust, potential, and limited practical quantum advantage, then evaluates a simulated quantum circuit Born machine against several classical generators using distinct query-budget and cost-evaluation-budget tracks. The QCBM performs best in the smallest-data fixed-query regime and balances quality with validity, but the result remains conditional on a synthetic parity task, exact simulation, omitted training cost, and an incomplete classical frontier. The framework therefore contributes a disciplined comparison method and a candidate advantage regime rather than a hardware demonstration.

[[Random-Circuit Quantum Advantage Verification]] applies the same adversarial standard to linear cross-entropy benchmarking. XEB can track fidelity when weak independent errors are distributed homogeneously, but localized or correlated errors disturb the relation, and a classical algorithm can partition a circuit, simulate the pieces, and amplify residual correlation through top-$k$ selection. Doubled-Hilbert-space, diffusion-reaction, and Ising-model descriptions show why XEB and fidelity agree near equilibrium yet diverge around localized sinks. The result is a general verification lesson: an efficiently measured score must be tested against algorithms that optimize the score itself, not only against algorithms that faithfully simulate the intended experiment.

[[Quantum Generative Architecture Performance]] examines the lower-level choices that determine whether such a generator trains and samples effectively. Continuous circuits produce expectation-valued features with few qubits but many measurements, whereas discrete circuits consume precision qubits and return one grid sample per shot. Applying a probability integral transform and enforcing uniform marginals with a copula circuit usually matters more than choosing QCBM or QGAN training, and the strongest low-dimensional fits use far fewer parameters than the classical baselines. Those counts remain incomplete resource measures: noiseless simulation favors continuous readout, grid resolution grows circuit width, generalization is not evaluated, and industrial-scale estimates quickly exceed near-term hardware.

[[Quantum Invertible Generative Models]] approach density estimation through a reversible map rather than direct Born sampling or adversarial training. Unitary circuit layers can be inverted with their adjoints, but expectation measurement discards state information, so a hybrid inverse-state-preparation module learns to reconstruct a state that returns to the original input. Negative log-likelihood, reconstruction, and maximum-mean-discrepancy terms divide the work of fitting a latent density, enforcing functional invertibility, and guiding early optimization. A simulated five-qubit collider study suggests that this construction can match much larger classical invertible networks by parameter count, while also showing why the claim remains architectural: most parameters are classical, full-Jacobian and loss evaluations require repeated circuit measurements, and no noisy hardware or end-to-end resource advantage is demonstrated.

[[Quantum State Diffusion Models]] generate distributions of individual quantum states rather than only classical samples or an ensemble-average density operator. A scrambling circuit creates a path from structured states to Haar-like noise, and measurement-conditioned reverse circuits learn that path one shallow segment at a time. This staging supplies intermediate targets and limits each local optimization depth, while fidelity-kernel MMD and Wasserstein transport compare generated and target ensembles. Small simulations reproduce clustered states, correlated coherent errors, ferromagnetic Ising states, and a one-qubit ring, but their limitations are equally instructive: fidelity kernels can miss topology, pairwise SWAP tests create measurement overhead, the generalization law is empirical, and the reported advantages over direct transport and QuGAN remain confined to noiseless one- to four-qubit tasks.

The most direct meeting of quantum computation and fundamental physics appears in [[Quantum Computing for High-Energy Physics]]. Hamiltonian quantum simulation can avoid the sampling sign problem that obstructs important real-time, finite-density, and topological calculations in lattice field theory. Near-term benchmarks therefore use lower-dimensional gauge theories, collective neutrino oscillations, and carefully chosen resource scales as steps toward realistic Standard Model simulations. Experimental high-energy physics poses a complementary challenge: collider measurements are classical, high-dimensional, and abundant, so quantum learning must justify the cost of compression, state preparation, repeated measurements, and noise mitigation. Candidate tasks include rare-signal searches, track and jet reconstruction, inference of physical parameters, and generative detector simulation.

Across these child topics, the central tradeoff is representation versus accessibility. A quantum state can encode a vast Hilbert space, but preparing the right state, preserving its symmetries, optimizing its circuit, and extracting accurate observables can consume the apparent advantage. A model may be expressive yet untrainable, shallow yet too restricted, or theoretically efficient while relying on costly data loading. Useful quantum work therefore couples physical structure to algorithm design: conservation laws shape ansatze, hardware connectivity shapes decompositions, error processes shape circuit depth, and the intended observable shapes measurement strategy.

Quantum physics and cosmology consequently form more than a collection of counterintuitive effects. They provide the concepts that explain why quantum information behaves differently, the mathematical models that define physically meaningful computations, and the scientific problems against which quantum hardware can be tested. The progression from foundational evidence to adaptive circuits, structured learning systems, and high-energy simulations shows how basic principles become computational tools without erasing the experimental constraints that give those tools meaning.
