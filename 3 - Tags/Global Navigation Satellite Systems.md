# Global Navigation Satellite Systems

## Overview Chapter

Modern satellite navigation did not begin with satellites. [[Pre-Satellite Navigation Systems]] established the problems and design patterns that later systems inherited. Quartz oscillators made time portable and repeatable, radio stations distributed that time over long distances, and direction-finding beacons converted radio reception into a line of position. VOR organized air routes around measured bearings. LORAN and Omega compared synchronized signals from separated transmitters, demonstrating that a passive receiver could determine position without revealing itself. Inertial systems solved a different problem by integrating motion internally, making them independent of external transmitters but vulnerable to accumulated drift. These systems supplied both the technical vocabulary and the operational demands against which satellite navigation was judged.

[[Satellite Navigation Principles]] explains the measurement chain behind a navigation fix. A satellite must transmit a recognizable signal from a position that the receiver can reconstruct. Pseudorandom codes let many satellites share frequencies while allowing a receiver to correlate one code at a time. The displacement needed to align the received and locally generated codes reveals signal travel time, which becomes a measured range after multiplication by the speed of light. Three ranges would determine a three-dimensional position if the receiver clock were perfect; a fourth satellite lets the receiver estimate its own clock bias as an additional unknown. Ephemeris and navigation-message data describe satellite position, clock health, and constellation state, while Doppler and repeated solutions add velocity. The result is a position, velocity, and time solution rather than a point delivered directly by a satellite.

Those principles are embodied institutionally and physically in [[GPS System Architecture]]. GPS divides responsibility among a space segment, a control segment, and a user segment. Medium-Earth-orbit satellites carry atomic clocks and broadcast coded L-band signals. Ground stations observe the satellites, estimate their orbits and clock behavior, predict future states, and upload corrections. Receivers perform the final correlations and calculations. Coarse/acquisition and precise codes support different access and security requirements, while common carrier frequencies and distinct codes allow satellites to share spectrum. This architecture combines continuous global coverage, passive use, precise time, and rapid three-dimensional fixes, but it also depends on a carefully maintained connection between orbital prediction, synchronized clocks, signal design, and receiver computation.

[[GPS Program History and Policy]] shows that this architecture was selected rather than inevitable. Transit proved that satellite navigation was practical by using Doppler shifts for submarine positioning. The Navy's Timation experiments tested spaceborne clocks and time transfer, while Air Force Project 621B developed pseudorandom coding and an alternative constellation concept. The 1973 Joint Program Office combined these traditions into a common system, and validation satellites and receivers demonstrated the design before full deployment. Policy decisions shaped the system just as strongly as engineering did. Selective Availability intentionally reduced civil accuracy, anti-spoofing protected military codes, the destruction of Korean Air Lines Flight 007 prompted a promise of future civil access, and later executive decisions removed intentional degradation. GPS consequently became a jointly governed civil-military utility whose users often extend far beyond the missions that originally funded it.

Once signals and receivers became available, [[GPS Commercialization and Applications]] expanded the meaning of navigation. Falling microelectronics costs transformed experimental military equipment into survey instruments, handheld receivers, automobile navigators, aviation aids, and embedded components. Differential correction turned a known reference position into a local error estimate, enabling meter-, decimeter-, or even centimeter-scale work. Agriculture used repeatable positioning to guide machinery and manage fields; fleet systems joined location with communications and dispatch; aviation paired satellite measurements with augmentation and integrity services. Precise timing became as consequential as coordinates because telecommunications and other distributed infrastructure rely on a common time base. Smartphones then combined GPS with maps, cellular positioning, inertial sensors, traffic services, and local search, making satellite navigation an often-invisible layer beneath everyday software.

GPS is one member of the larger family organized in [[Global Navigation Satellite Constellations]]. Russia's GLONASS, Europe's Galileo, China's BeiDou, India's regional system, and Japan's QZSS express different mixtures of global ambition, regional coverage, military independence, civil control, and augmentation. WAAS and EGNOS illustrate how surveyed ground networks and geostationary broadcasts can improve accuracy and provide operational assurance over a region. Multiconstellation receivers can select measurements from more satellites and improve availability where buildings, terrain, or latitude restrict the sky view. Interoperability therefore provides practical benefits, but it is also political: maintaining an independent constellation prevents a nation or region from relying entirely on another government's promise of continued service.

The same ubiquity creates the risks collected in [[GPS Accuracy Integrity and Resilience]]. Satellite geometry can magnify range errors, and the ionosphere changes signal travel time. Solar activity can disturb that medium or damage space hardware. Physical attacks threaten satellites and control facilities, while low received power makes civil signals susceptible to local jamming. Spoofing is more subtle because a false but plausible signal can mislead a receiver rather than merely deny service. Resilience comes from multiple layers: protected codes such as M-code, monitoring and integrity checks, independent constellations, inertial sensors that bridge outages, and dissimilar terrestrial systems such as enhanced LORAN. No single measure removes dependence; each changes which failure modes remain common.

Together these topics show GPS as a coordinated measurement system and a social infrastructure. Accurate clocks have value only when their signals can be identified, their transmitting positions are known, propagation errors are managed, and receivers can solve the resulting equations. Technical performance alone is also insufficient: governments must fund and govern constellations, civil and military users must share signals, manufacturers must build affordable receivers, and operators must plan for disruption. Global navigation satellite systems succeed because orbital mechanics, radio engineering, computation, standards, public policy, and applications reinforce one another. Their apparent simplicity at the user interface conceals a global chain of clocks, codes, predictions, corrections, and institutional commitments.
