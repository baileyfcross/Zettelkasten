# AI Ethics

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

AI Ethics organizes the moral, social, political, and environmental questions raised by the design and use of artificial intelligence. It is a chapter-tier topic: focused notes link to the child tags below rather than directly to this page.

## Overview Chapter

Artificial intelligence is ethically significant not because a machine must first become conscious or superhuman, but because present systems already influence decisions, opportunities, relationships, and environments. AI is never only an isolated algorithm. It is part of a sociotechnical arrangement that includes data collection, model design, infrastructure, organizations, users, affected communities, and the institutions that decide where a system will be used. Ethical analysis therefore has to move between technical details and questions about responsibility, justice, power, human flourishing, and the conditions of life on Earth.

### Futures, narratives, and human identity

[[AI Futures and Human Identity]] examines the stories and philosophical assumptions through which people understand intelligent machines. Speculation about general intelligence, superintelligence, an intelligence explosion, or a technological singularity can reveal genuine control problems, but it can also draw attention away from current harms. Narratives such as the Golem, Frankenstein, and the machine uprising frame AI as either a created servant or a monster that escapes its maker. These stories are not neutral predictions: they influence which risks receive funding, which policies appear urgent, and whether creators accept responsibility for what they build.

Questions about AI also expose assumptions about human beings. Classical arguments emphasize meaning, tacit knowledge, embodiment, and situated experience as limits on purely formal computation. Transhumanism hopes to overcome bodily and cognitive limits through technology, while posthumanism challenges the idea that humanity must remain the measure of every form of intelligence and value. A relational view adds that humans and technologies shape one another through use. The ethical task is therefore not simply to preserve a fixed human essence, but to ask what kinds of people and societies particular technologies help to produce.

### Machines as moral agents and patients

[[Machine Moral Status]] separates two questions that are often conflated. Moral agency asks whether an AI can make morally relevant choices or bear responsibility; moral patiency asks whether an AI can itself be wronged. A system may display functional morality by following constraints or adapting its behavior without possessing the understanding, freedom, or emotional life associated with full moral agency. For current systems, attributing responsibility to the machine can obscure the responsibilities of designers, deployers, owners, and institutions.

Moral patiency is also unsettled. One approach bases status on internal capacities such as sentience. Another treats duties toward machines as indirect duties because cruelty to a humanlike robot may shape human character or social behavior. A relational approach focuses on how a being enters social practices and is addressed by people, rather than demanding proof of hidden internal properties first. Human empathy toward robots makes these questions practically important even before machine consciousness is established. The debate also reveals anthropocentrism: it asks whether human resemblance is the only route to moral consideration.

### Data, privacy, and digital power

[[Data Ethics and Digital Power]] follows AI through the data pipeline. Capturing, cleaning, combining, selecting, modeling, interpreting, and acting on data all require choices. A dataset is an abstraction rather than a transparent copy of reality, and a large dataset can remain unrepresentative. Correlation may be mistaken for causation, repurposed data may escape the context in which consent was given, and apparently harmless variables may reveal sensitive traits when combined.

Privacy is therefore more than secrecy. It includes meaningful control over collection and use, the ability to understand automated processing, and protection against surveillance, profiling, manipulation, and exploitation. The data economy also depends on hidden labor: people label examples, moderate content, and generate behavioral traces that become productive inputs. Bots and synthetic media can erode trust by making identity and evidence harder to verify. As dependence on connected systems grows, failures and attacks on infrastructure become human vulnerabilities rather than merely technical inconveniences.

### Responsibility and intelligible decisions

[[AI Responsibility and Explainability]] begins from the idea that responsibility normally requires both control and knowledge. AI complicates these conditions because actions emerge from long development histories, distributed teams, changing data, interacting components, and decisions made at machine speed. This produces responsibility gaps, the problem of many hands, and the problem of many things. None of these should become an excuse for treating harmful outcomes as nobody's responsibility. Duties of care, organizational accountability, traceability, and auditability distribute obligations across the people and institutions able to prevent, investigate, or remedy harm.

Explainability supports answerability. A black-box model may be difficult even for its developers to interpret, but merely publishing code does not necessarily explain an individual decision. Useful explanations are social and audience-sensitive: a developer, regulator, and affected person may need different levels of detail. The right to explanation, records of system behavior, and an ethical black box can help reconstruct why a result occurred. Transparency must therefore be designed around the questions people are entitled to ask, not treated as indiscriminate disclosure.

### Bias, fairness, and justice

[[Algorithmic Fairness and Justice]] addresses the way automated decisions can reproduce or intensify unequal treatment. Bias can enter through unrepresentative training data, labels, proxy variables, model objectives, deployment choices, historical discrimination, or automation bias among users. Predictive policing illustrates a feedback loop: concentrating enforcement in one area produces more recorded incidents there, which then appears to justify still more enforcement. Removing a protected attribute does not guarantee fairness because other variables can encode much the same social pattern.

Fairness is not a single technical metric. Different definitions can conflict, and disagreement often reflects competing theories of justice. Treating everyone identically may preserve an unequal starting point, while corrective measures such as affirmative action intentionally use group membership to address prior disadvantage. Ethical work must therefore connect quantitative tests to political judgment, affected communities, and the distribution of benefits and burdens. Diverse teams and bias-mitigation techniques help, but they cannot make a contested social decision value-free.

### Governance and responsible innovation

[[AI Governance and Responsible Innovation]] turns ethical principles into institutions and practices. Policy proposals converge around human dignity, autonomy, fairness, privacy, safety, accountability, explicability, and sustainability. Trustworthy AI requires more than publishing a list. Ethics by design, value-sensitive design, privacy by design, testing, certification, standards, education, and legal rules provide different ways to operationalize these commitments. Product liability and duties of care may assign responsibility without pretending a machine is a legal or moral person.

Good governance is both proactive and participatory. Considering consequences early can prevent harmful systems from becoming entrenched, but designers cannot foresee every effect. Stakeholder participation brings practical knowledge and the voices of people who will bear risk into the process. It also limits the ability of powerful firms to write their own rules. Responsible innovation must retain the option to delay or reject an application; otherwise it can become a method for making predetermined development appear acceptable. Positive ethics complements limits by asking what kind of good life and good society AI should support.

### Work, inequality, and the environment

[[AI Labor and Environmental Ethics]] expands the frame beyond individual users. Automation can remove tasks, transform occupations, concentrate wealth, and weaken the meaning or security people derive from work. Human-AI collaboration may preserve judgment and create useful divisions of labor, while proposals such as a universal basic income attempt to separate subsistence from employment. Yet the visible system may conceal click workers, content moderators, and other people whose labor makes apparently autonomous AI possible. The distribution of gains and risks is therefore an ethical property of the system.

AI also consumes electricity, materials, infrastructure, and human attention. It can assist climate modeling, energy coordination, and environmental monitoring, while simultaneously increasing energy demand and electronic waste. Sustainable AI evaluates both use and production rather than treating computation as immaterial. Techno-solutionism is dangerous when it substitutes prediction and optimization for political choices about priorities, values, and distribution. In the Anthropocene, delegating planetary management to machines could intensify human control over nature while reducing autonomy. Intelligence can help identify patterns, but practical wisdom about how to live with other people, nonhuman beings, and the Earth cannot simply be delegated.

Together, these topics show that AI ethics is a continuing practice of connecting technical choices to their human and nonhuman consequences. It asks not only whether a system works, but who defines success, who gains, who bears the risk, who can demand an explanation, and whether the resulting world is worth building.
