# Quantum Computing

Quantum computing is the chapter-level topic for qubit representations, processor hardware, quantum algorithms, circuit construction, noise mitigation, variational optimization, quantum machine learning, generative modeling, and application-specific resource analysis.

This chapter synthesizes only the existing Full Notes gathered by its child tags and the local source material listed in those notes' References sections. It does not introduce claims from external material.

## Overview Chapter

Quantum computing joins a mathematical description of information with a physical method for preparing, transforming, and measuring that information. Its central promise does not come from labeling an ordinary calculation as quantum. It comes from arranging amplitudes, interference, entanglement, and measurement so that a physically realizable circuit exposes structure useful to a particular problem. The notes collected in this chapter show that every such proposal has several inseparable layers: a state representation, an allowed set of transformations, an implementation on hardware, a strategy for learning from measurements, and evidence that the complete procedure improves on available alternatives.

### Information, circuits, hardware, and evidence

[[Quantum Information and Circuit Foundations]] provides the common language for the rest of the chapter. A qubit is represented by a normalized complex state, multi-qubit systems are formed through tensor products, and measurement turns amplitudes into classical outcomes in a selected basis. Separability and entanglement distinguish independently describable subsystems from states with nonclassical joint correlations. Unitary gates preserve normalization and reversibility, while controlled operations create the interactions needed for logic, entanglement, and compact state preparation. These ideas explain what a circuit denotes before questions of speed or usefulness can be asked.

That abstract circuit must be realized as a controlled physical system. [[Superconducting Qubit Engineering]] follows the path from Josephson-junction circuit design to addressable qubits, coupling, pulse control, coherence protection, and dispersive readout. Transmon and fluxonium designs make different compromises among anharmonicity, tunability, and sensitivity to noise. Microwave shaping, dynamical decoupling, resonators, filters, and parametric amplification show that state preparation and measurement are not separate from computation: the processor's topology, environment, control schedule, and readout chain jointly determine the circuit that can actually run.

[[Quantum Algorithm Execution and Verification]] connects canonical algorithms to those engineering constraints. Grover search uses oracle phases and amplitude amplification, while Shor factoring places quantum period finding inside a larger classical procedure. Logical circuits then have to be compiled into native gates, routed through available connectivity, scheduled within coherence limits, and checked against simulations or tractable subinstances. Qubit count alone is therefore insufficient; fidelity, connectivity, calibration, control speed, and error correction determine whether a nominal algorithm is an executable one. Fault-tolerant and measurement-based models further demonstrate that the architecture of execution changes the meaning of the resource estimate.

[[Quantum and Post-Quantum Cryptography]] connects those information principles to secure communication. Quantum key distribution prepares and measures states in selected bases, uses a classical channel to reconcile compatible positions, and treats measurement disturbance as evidence that an interceptor may be present. BB84 then applies error checking and privacy amplification before the parties retain a shared key. The same topic follows the pressure quantum algorithms place on classical public-key assumptions and introduces lattice problems and NTRU as a post-quantum design direction.

Claims of advantage require measurements that remain informative under both physical errors and classical attack. [[Random-Circuit Quantum Advantage Verification]] studies linear cross-entropy benchmarking as one such measurement. Its relation to circuit fidelity is strongest under suitably distributed weak errors, but localized or correlated errors can change that relation. Classical partitioning and top-selection strategies can also raise the score without faithfully simulating the entire circuit. Verification must therefore test a metric against procedures designed to optimize that metric, not only against procedures that attempt full simulation.

### Optimization encodings and structured algorithms

Some quantum workflows begin with a classical discrete objective rather than a gate sequence. [[Quantum Annealing and QUBO Programming]] shows how binary variables and constraints become Ising fields and couplings or a quadratic unconstrained binary objective. Penalty functions raise the energy of invalid assignments, reusable equality and cardinality terms assemble larger models, and frustration expresses incompatible local preferences through the best available global compromise. The physical annealing schedule then introduces its own questions about tunneling, thermal relaxation, read counts, and pauses. Correct encoding is necessary, but it does not guarantee that the device samples a useful minimum.

[[Constraint-Preserving QAOA]] approaches related combinatorial problems through alternating gate-model operators. A cost phase encodes the objective, while the mixer and the initial state are chosen so that evolution remains within the feasible subspace. One-hot graph-coloring mixers and neighbor-controlled rotations for independent-set problems avoid spending most of the circuit on invalid assignments. Ring, complete-graph, simultaneous, and partitioned constructions trade interaction range, depth, and connectivity within the feasible space. In this setting, constraint handling is part of the quantum dynamics rather than a penalty added after circuit design.

Financial algorithms illustrate a different use of structured transformations. [[Quantum Signal Processing for Derivative Pricing]] represents bounded functions through polynomial transformations of amplitudes, reducing reliance on large reversible-arithmetic circuits for some payoff calculations. Comparator constructions and Chebyshev approximations can lower estimated logical resources, although the remaining requirements still depend on fault-tolerant non-Clifford gates, logical qubits, and a payoff reducible to the chosen transformed variable. [[Quantum Risk Analysis of Derivative Portfolios]] then places pricing oracles inside coherent market scenarios to estimate value at risk and conditional value at risk. The analysis compares amplitude-estimation and signal-processing constructions while keeping state preparation and high-dimensional scenario loading visible as possible resource bottlenecks.

[[Quantum Chebyshev Modeling]] uses the same polynomial family as a feature representation rather than only as an arithmetic shortcut. Chebyshev values become amplitudes in an orthonormal latent basis, and a transform connects that basis to computational bitstrings. The resulting circuits support function learning, differentiation with respect to inputs, and projective sampling. Ancilla management, nonuniform nodes, variational trainability, and multivariable scaling remain part of the implementation cost, so the basis choice cannot be separated from how the trained model will be queried.

### Building, adapting, and optimizing circuits

Parameterized circuits turn circuit execution into a hybrid optimization problem. [[Variable-Structure Variational Quantum Circuits]] allows both gate arrangement and continuous angles to change, adding or removing identity-compiling blocks in search of an ansatz suited to the device and task. The approach addresses a basic tension: excessive depth increases exposure to noise, while insufficient structure prevents the circuit from representing the desired state or transformation. [[Hierarchical Multigrid Variational Quantum Algorithms]] makes this adaptation coarse-to-fine, transferring parameters and refining circuits as a problem moves through a hierarchy of resolutions. The hierarchy uses smaller instances to guide larger ones instead of restarting each optimization independently.

Circuit structure can also be generated by classical search models. [[Genetic Quantum Circuit Optimization]] uses population-based evolution over circuit gates, topology, parameters, and hardware-aware objectives. [[Diffusion Models for Quantum Circuit Synthesis]] represents circuits as tensors and learns a conditional denoising process that produces circuits from noisy representations, with fine-tuning used to improve generated candidates. Both approaches broaden structure search beyond a fixed ansatz, but their usefulness is governed by representation choices, fitness or conditioning signals, and the cost of checking the proposed circuits.

[[Policy-Gradient Variational Quantum Compilation]] formulates shallow approximate synthesis as sequential decision-making. A stochastic policy proposes parameter updates, receives fidelity-based rewards, and can cross flat or misleading regions that hinder a strictly local optimizer. [[Meta-Learned Variational Quantum Optimization]] instead trains a recurrent classical optimizer across related circuit problems. At inference time it uses a short history of parameters and measured expectations to locate a promising basin before a conventional local method refines the solution. Its transfer behavior depends on compatible parameter dimensions and problem structure, while meta-training itself can require a large simulation budget.

Prior knowledge can reduce the search space before optimization. [[Symmetry-Equivariant Variational Quantum Learning]] aligns data embeddings, trainable generators, boundary states, observables, and parameter sharing with known transformations or conserved sectors. Group-twirled generators and invariant measurements prevent symmetry-related inputs from being treated inconsistently. This can reduce parameters and improve generalization or gradients, but it may also remove useful generators or create nonlocal hardware decompositions. [[Hamiltonian Variational Ansatz Trainability]] studies the same problem through gradient scaling, initialization, effective-Hamiltonian approximations, and parameter sharing, asking which Hamiltonian-inspired structures retain usable optimization signals as circuits grow.

Gradient estimation supplies those signals. [[Quantum Circuit Gradient Rules]] explains when shifted circuit evaluations give exact derivatives, how asymmetric shifts mix first and second derivatives, and why apparent one-sided shortcuts cannot be universal. It also separates exact shift identities from biased finite differences and makes evaluation count part of the derivative method. [[Variational Quantum Optimizer Benchmarking]] then compares optimizers across VQE, QAOA, classification, and regression tasks. Its results show why step count, circuit-evaluation count, shot count, learning-rate selection, noise assumptions, and random starts must be reported together; an optimizer ranking under one accounting rule is not automatically a general hierarchy.

Geometry-aware methods change the update rather than only the derivative estimator. [[Natural-Gradient Optimization of Optical Quantum Circuits]] uses the Fubini-Study metric and complex-variable derivatives to measure movement in prepared-state space for photonic circuits. Layerwise approximations and regularized inverses make the update feasible for small state-preparation demonstrations. [[Stochastic Quantum Natural-Gradient Optimization]] estimates related geometric information from a constant number of randomly perturbed state overlaps, averages low-rank samples, and regularizes the resulting preconditioner. It can reduce the quadratic element-by-element cost of a full quantum Fisher matrix, but overlap circuits, sampling variance, inversion bias, and regularization remain part of its resource profile.

### Noise mitigation and scalable estimation

Near-term circuit optimization is inseparable from finite-shot noise. [[Error-Mitigation Trainability of Variational Quantum Algorithms]] asks whether a protocol that improves an expectation value also improves an optimizer's ability to distinguish nearby parameters. Zero-noise extrapolation, virtual distillation, probabilistic cancellation, and regression-based corrections can reduce bias, yet their sampling overhead may erase the recovered signal. The relevant test is therefore finite-sample landscape resolvability rather than accuracy in an unlimited-shot limit.

The child studies sharpen that general comparison. [[Linear-Rescaling Error Mitigation for VQE]] calibrates a linear correction for damped variational-eigensolver observables and tests where that correction fails as depth and experimental conditions change. [[Randomized Compiling and Zero-Noise Extrapolation]] first tailors coherent errors toward stochastic Pauli noise so that scaled-noise measurements better match the assumptions behind extrapolation. Finite randomization, realistic device noise, calibration, and the circuit controls used in the experiment determine how much of the ideal correction survives.

[[Scalable Error-Mitigated Quantum Simulation]] extends the question to larger observable-estimation circuits. Sparse Pauli-Lindblad models make noise learning an inverse problem, while probabilistic cancellation, extrapolation, and tensor-network error mitigation expose different sampling exponents and systematic errors. Tensor-network error mitigation moves inverse noise onto the observable and can approach a sampling lower bound, but its bond dimension introduces a classical compression threshold and residual bias. Forecasts for deeper, wider circuits consequently depend on low error rates, stable calibration, contraction resources, and comparison with strong universal classical simulation.

### Quantum representations for learning

Quantum machine learning begins with the decision of how classical features or quantum states enter a circuit. [[Circuit-Centric Quantum Classification]] gathers classifiers based on amplitude encodings, image-state representations, and repeated data-dependent rotations. A parameterized circuit transforms the encoded state and measurements become class predictions. The encoding, ansatz, observable, and data dimension jointly determine whether the circuit is compact, trainable, and hardware-compatible.

Several child topics make those choices concrete. [[Single-Qubit Data Re-Uploading Classifiers]] repeatedly inserts classical features into rotations of one qubit and compares the final state with label states on the Bloch sphere. [[Unary-Encoded Quantum Neural Networks]] uses the single-excitation subspace so that beam-splitter-like circuits perform linear operations with one real feature per qubit. [[Variational Quantum Image Classification]] approximates structured pixel encodings, applies hardware-aware trainable circuits, and derives multiple classes from measured bitstrings. These designs exchange width, state-preparation complexity, expressivity, and measurement cost in different ways rather than supplying a single universal encoding.

Hierarchical circuits add a strong architectural bias. [[Quantum Convolutional Neural Networks]] alternate local unitaries with measurement-conditioned pooling, progressively reducing the active system while learning phase recognition, effective observables, or error-correcting behavior. [[Quantum and Hybrid Convolutional Image Classification]] applies related circuit components to image patches, compressed features, or amplitude-encoded images, sometimes combining quantum layers with classical convolutional processing. Circuit placement, entanglement, decoding, and measurement design determine whether the quantum component contributes more than a costly feature transformation.

State compression provides another representation strategy. [[Quantum Autoencoding and State Compression]] trains variational encoders to concentrate low-rank quantum-state information into a smaller subsystem while preserving useful spectral or downstream information. [[Enhanced Feature Quantum Autoencoders]] adds state-dependent model features to a shared trainable circuit so that compression can adapt to individual inputs without replacing the common encoder. Both topics make reconstruction, retained information, ansatz capacity, and the eventual use of the compressed state part of the evaluation.

Regularization and optical implementations broaden the model family. [[Quantum Dropout Regularization]] randomly removes parameterized circuit operations during training to reduce co-adaptation in overparameterized quantum neural networks. [[Quantum Optical Neurons]] encode inputs and weights into quantum optical states, use interference to create an overlap-like computation, and obtain predictions from aggregate detector statistics. [[Quantum-Deformed Probabilistic Neural Networks]] promotes binary activations and weights into correlated quantum degrees of freedom before returning a thresholded classical output; low-entanglement restrictions make some versions classically simulable and clarify that altered model structure is not by itself evidence of computational advantage.

Domain structure remains important after the model is defined. [[Quantum Natural Language Processing for Financial Sentiment]] compares recurrent cells whose internal subnetworks are variational circuits with grammar-aware constructions that turn parsed composition into circuit connectivity. The linked notes keep parser availability, postselection, circuit simulation, dataset construction, and unequal training budgets in the comparison, showing how software and experimental design can dominate the apparent distinction between models.

### Generative models and application benchmarks

Quantum generative modeling divides into continuous expectation-based circuits, discrete bitstring samplers, adversarial systems, reversible models, and state-generating processes. [[Quantum Generative Architecture Performance]] compares continuous, discrete, and copula-structured designs together with preprocessing, QCBM and QGAN objectives, parameter counts, shot costs, grid resolution, and simulation assumptions. Uniformizing marginals through probability-integral transforms can matter as much as the nominal training family. Parameter efficiency alone remains incomplete when measurement load, generalization, noise, and data preparation are omitted.

[[Quantum Generative Model Advantage Benchmarking]] defines evidence tiers and resource-constrained comparison tracks before declaring an advantage. A quantum circuit Born machine can perform well in a small-data, fixed-query regime on a structured synthetic task, but that result remains conditional on exact simulation, omitted training costs, and the set of classical baselines. The contribution is therefore both a candidate regime and a method for preventing incomparable query, training, and evaluation budgets from being collapsed into one score.

Hardware-oriented adversarial models expose further tradeoffs. [[Experimental Quantum GAN Image Generation]] divides image generation across small patch circuits or uses index qubits in a proposed coherent-batch construction. Superconducting experiments can produce small recognizable outputs, while ancilla postselection, parameter-shift measurements, state preparation, batch size, and hardware noise remain part of the resource claim. [[Variational Quantum Wasserstein Anomaly Detection]] places a variational circuit in the generator of a Wasserstein AnoGAN while leaving the critic and high-dimensional data path classical. Its benchmark, convergence behavior, finite-shot delay, and ansatz ablations distinguish matching a classical score from demonstrating an end-to-end quantum benefit.

Other generators emphasize reversibility or distributions over quantum states. [[Quantum Invertible Generative Models]] combines invertible unitary layers with a learned reconstruction module because expectation measurement discards state information needed for a simple classical inverse. Likelihood, reconstruction, and distribution-matching losses divide the work among density fitting, functional invertibility, and early optimization. [[Quantum State Diffusion Models]] instead learns ensembles of individual quantum states through a scrambling forward process and staged measurement-conditioned reverse circuits. Fidelity-kernel and transport distances compare generated ensembles, while shallow simulations reveal limitations in topology sensitivity, pairwise test overhead, and scaling evidence.

High-energy physics supplies application-specific tests for both simulation and generation. [[Quantum Computing for High-Energy Physics]] covers Hamiltonian simulation of field dynamics, collective-neutrino models, and collider-data workflows. It keeps encoding, state preparation, measurement, error mitigation, compression, and classical competition visible when moving from reduced benchmarks toward realistic calculations. [[A-qGAN Anomaly Detection in High-Energy Physics]] trains a quantum generative adversarial model of background-like collider data and combines distribution learning with anomaly scores under simulation and hardware tests. [[Style-qGAN Monte Carlo Event Generation]] repeatedly injects latent variables into a variational circuit and compares generated analytic or collider distributions across noiseless, noisy, superconducting, and trapped-ion settings. These applications show why a quantum model must be evaluated as a full data pipeline rather than only as a circuit diagram.

Across the collected topics, the recurring issue is access to useful structure. Quantum states can represent large spaces, but the desired state must be prepared, the circuit must respect hardware and problem symmetries, optimization signals must survive finite sampling, and the final observable must expose the answer without excessive reconstruction. A shallow circuit can be too restricted; an expressive circuit can be untrainable; a corrected estimate can require too many shots; and a compact parameterization can hide expensive loading or measurement. The chapter therefore treats quantum computing as a linked stack of representations, algorithms, controls, estimators, and verification methods. Progress at one layer is meaningful only when the assumptions and costs passed to the other layers remain explicit.
