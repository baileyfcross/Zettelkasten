# Game Foundations

Parent topics: [[Game Design]] · [[Computer Science]]

Game Foundations is an index for the defining structures and expressive forms of games. Full Notes should use the more focused topics below.

## Overview Chapter

Games are designed experiences produced by the interaction of rules, player actions, goals, representations, and consequences. The foundational topics in this section explain how a game becomes both a formal system and a lived activity. They move from the mechanics that define possibility to the experiences and stories that players construct while acting within that possibility.

### The playable system

[[Game Mechanics and Rules]] establishes the formal core. Rules define valid states and transformations; mechanics expose recurring ways for the player to cause those transformations. A mechanic becomes playable when the player can understand the available action, perform it through an interface, and perceive how the game responded. Rules that never affect a decision or cannot be inferred from feedback remain inert from the player's point of view.

[[Game Interactivity]] expands this action-response relationship into a loop. Players interpret the current state, form an intention, act, observe feedback, and update their understanding. The loop operates at several scales. A button press may produce immediate movement, a strategic choice may alter a match minutes later, and a social action may change a community over months. Designing interactivity means connecting these timescales so that local actions remain legible while supporting larger patterns.

The space in which these patterns occur is organized through [[Level and Scene Design]]. Levels establish spatial routes, introduce rules, control pacing, stage encounters, and place meaningful decisions. A scene can teach by arranging consequences so that the player discovers a relationship through action. Its shape should serve a purpose in the larger experience rather than exist as an isolated obstacle.

### Communicating meaning

[[Game Presentation]] makes the playable system perceptible. Camera, composition, animation, sound, silhouette, and visual motif direct attention and explain changes in state. Presentation also establishes context: the same abstract rule can feel comic, threatening, heroic, or mundane depending on how it is represented. Strong presentation aligns sensory emphasis with mechanical importance so that players notice what they need without being overwhelmed.

[[Game Challenge Design]] gives actions resistance. A challenge asks the player to form and execute a response under constraints. Its difficulty depends not only on numerical strength but also on available information, required precision, prior learning, and the cost of failure. A productive challenge sits near the player's developing skill, supports diagnosis after failure, and permits improvement rather than relying only on surprise.

[[Game Rewards and Consequences]] gives outcomes weight. Scores, achievements, resources, checkpoints, punishment, and systemic changes can mark progress or reshape future choices. An effective reward supports the desired play instead of distracting from it. Consequences become meaningful when players can connect them to prior decisions and use them to guide later action.

### Experience and story

The ultimate effect of these structures is addressed by [[Player Experience Design]]. Players bring goals, motivations, skills, expectations, and social contexts that the formal system cannot completely determine. Engagement and flow arise when goals are understandable, feedback is timely, and challenge develops with competence. Onboarding helps players build a useful mental model, while opportunities for expression let different players make the system personally meaningful.

Narrative can be supplied through authored content or discovered through play. [[Authored Game Narrative]] uses lore, themes, characters, cutscenes, quests, and branching sequences to construct deliberate dramatic forms. It gives designers control over emphasis and pacing, although every branch increases the content that must be written, built, and tested.

[[Emergent Game Narrative]] forms when rules and player choices produce events that participants interpret as a story. The designer supplies a coherent world of objects, agents, pressures, and consequences, but does not prescribe every sequence. The two forms can reinforce one another: authored material establishes stakes and meaning, while emergent events give players ownership of what happened.

Together these foundations show that a game is neither only software nor only a story. It is a designed possibility space communicated through sensory form and completed through player activity. Mechanics create potential, levels stage it, presentation reveals it, challenges and consequences shape its value, and players transform it into experience and narrative.
