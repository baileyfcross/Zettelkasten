# Augmented Reality Systems

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

Augmented Reality Systems is the chapter-level topic for computational systems that combine interactive virtual information with the physical world in real time and register that information in three dimensions. Full Notes should use one of the focused child topics below rather than linking directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Augmented reality links computation to the physical environment instead of replacing that environment with a wholly synthetic one. [[XR Environments]] places this relationship on a continuum that includes real environments, augmented reality, augmented virtuality, and virtual reality. An AR experience combines real and virtual information, responds interactively in real time, and keeps virtual content registered with physical locations or objects. Those requirements make AR a complete perception-action system rather than a visual effect: the system must sense the world, maintain spatial relationships, generate augmentations, present them through suitable devices, and let the user act while the environment continues to change.

The perceptual boundary is supplied by [[XR Display Systems]]. Optical see-through displays place generated light into a direct view of the world, whereas video see-through displays first capture the world and then composite it with virtual imagery. Handheld, head-worn, stationary, and projected arrangements each establish different geometric relationships among the user's eyes, the display, the camera, and the physical scene. Field of view, resolution, brightness, contrast, focus, occlusion, viewpoint offset, refresh rate, latency, ergonomics, and social acceptability determine whether the combination remains legible and comfortable. Audio and haptic output can add spatial or tactile information when visual augmentation alone is insufficient.

Every spatially anchored augmentation depends on [[Spatial Tracking and Registration]]. A tracker measures a geometric property relative to a frame of reference, but its measurements have accuracy, precision, resolution, update-rate, latency, jitter, and drift characteristics. Optical sensors can provide rich environment-relative measurements, while inertial and other mobile sensors respond to different physical phenomena. Sensor fusion combines their complementary evidence. Calibration establishes fixed relationships among cameras, displays, trackers, and objects; spatial registration aligns their coordinate systems at runtime; and temporal registration ensures that measurements and rendered output refer to compatible moments.

[[Augmented Reality Computer Vision]] extracts camera or object pose from images. Marker tracking detects a prepared target, derives a homography from its corners, and estimates the camera pose. Natural-feature methods instead detect interest points, create descriptors, match them to a model, reject inconsistent correspondences with robust estimation, and solve for pose. Incremental tracking follows features from frame to frame. Simultaneous localization and mapping builds a map while locating the camera within it, with bundle adjustment refining the estimates and loop closure correcting accumulated error. These methods make optical tracking useful where permanent external tracking infrastructure is unavailable.

Accurate pose is necessary but not sufficient for a believable composite. [[Augmented Reality Visual Coherence]] coordinates the geometry and appearance of real and virtual objects. Geometric registration establishes shared position, orientation, and occlusion, while photometric registration estimates illumination so virtual objects receive light and cast effects that agree with the scene. Image-based lighting, light probes, common illumination, differential rendering, and real-time global illumination address increasingly rich light transport. Diminished reality removes a real object by modeling what lies behind it, and camera simulation applies distortion, blur, noise, or vignetting to virtual imagery so it resembles the captured image.

The purpose of the composite is often to communicate information, which belongs to [[Situated Visualization]]. Situated information is attached to the physical objects or locations it describes. That direct relationship reduces the mental transformation between a separate display and the world, but it introduces data overload, interference, registration uncertainty, and changing viewpoints. Label placement and temporal coherence keep annotations legible as the camera moves. X-ray views, ghosting, explosion diagrams, and spatial distortion reveal hidden structure, while knowledge-based and spatial filters limit the display to information relevant to the task and viewpoint.

AR also makes the physical environment part of the control surface. [[Tangible and Multimodal Interaction]] includes tracked rigid objects, body motion, gestures, touch, physical props, tangible objects, paper, haptics, speech, and combinations of these channels. A magic lens places augmentation inside a movable viewing region, while a multi-view interface lets different displays or viewpoints reveal different parts of the same mixed space. The design challenge is to preserve useful real-world affordances while coordinating tracking, feedback, and the semantics of each input and output modality.

Creating spatial content is addressed by [[Augmented Reality Modeling and Authoring]]. Modeling techniques specify points, planes, volumes, appearance, or free-form geometry while letting the creator compare virtual content directly with the physical target. Semi-automatic reconstruction uses measurements and constraints to reduce difficult mobile input. Authoring goes beyond geometry to define actors, stages, stories, interactions, setup, and behavior. Real-world interfaces, hardware abstraction, reusable workflows, performance-based capture, plug-ins for existing engines, and web-based content approaches make it possible to create AR experiences without rebuilding the underlying technical stack for every application.

Movement through an environment connects spatial knowledge to visible guidance. [[Augmented Reality Navigation]] can annotate nearby points of interest, reveal an otherwise hidden destination, overlay a route on the world, or guide the user toward a required object or viewpoint. These cues reduce the transformation between a map and the traveler's egocentric view, but a design should still support orientation and cognitive-map formation. Multiple-perspective and transitional interfaces connect detailed first-person guidance with an overview while preserving the user's understanding of how the views relate.

The shared physical world makes AR a communication medium as well as an individual interface. [[Collaborative Augmented Reality]] allows co-located users to see and manipulate the same real and virtual objects from individual viewpoints. Remote arrangements can share video, virtual annotations, reconstructed geometry, gestures, or avatars. An asymmetric remote-expert system lets one participant observe the other's task environment and place guidance directly into that participant's view. Collaboration therefore depends on common spatial references, synchronization, awareness cues, and appropriate control over shared and private information.

Finally, [[Augmented Reality Software Architecture]] integrates the sensing, computation, rendering, interaction, and distribution required by the other topics. AR software must abstract changing hardware, preserve real-virtual consistency, represent semantic and geometric knowledge, and meet real-time scheduling requirements. Distributed objects and dataflow graphs isolate processing components and allow connections to cross hosts. Scene graphs organize real and virtual objects hierarchically, while dependency graphs express derived values. Declarative configuration, scripting, reflection, runtime inspection, and reconfiguration help developers assemble and debug systems whose devices and environments cannot be fully known at compile time.

Together, these topics describe AR as a coordinated pipeline and a medium. Displays determine what can be perceived; tracking and vision determine where content belongs; calibration and coherence determine whether it fits; visualization and interaction determine whether it is understandable and controllable; authoring determines what the experience means; navigation and collaboration place it in human activity; and architecture keeps the complete real-time system adaptable. Weakness in any one layer can break the apparent relationship between digital information and the physical world.
