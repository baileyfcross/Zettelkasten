# Visual Effects and Compositing

This chapter-level topic connects the six focused areas below into a visual-effects workflow. Full Notes link to the focused topics rather than directly to this parent.

## Overview Chapter

Visual effects can be understood as the controlled joining of evidence from a filmed shot with material that was photographed separately, painted, tracked, simulated, or rendered. A successful composite is not merely an image whose layers fit inside the frame. The layers must agree about camera motion, depth, light, color, atmosphere, surface texture, timing, and the shot's role in a larger scene. Jon Gress's *[digital] Visual Effects and Compositing* develops this idea through practical examples that repeatedly move between two-dimensional image work, three-dimensional scenes, and hybrid 2.5D arrangements. Each focused topic in this chapter handles a different part of that agreement.

[[Compositing and Keying]] begins with visibility. An alpha channel states where a foreground image contributes and where the background must remain visible; a holdout matte makes the complementary subtraction. Chroma keying estimates such an alpha from a deliberately chosen screen, while a clean-plate difference key can use a matching empty image as its reference. A good matte does not guarantee good color. Blue or green light can spill onto a subject, a keyer can damage its RGB data, and a sharp edge can expose where plates meet. Keeping the original subject pixels, correcting spill separately, and feathering only where needed preserve useful source information. Layer and node representations organize these operations differently, but neither eliminates the need to check the final merge.

The extraction must then survive movement. [[Rotoscopy and Motion Tracking]] uses shape animation to follow silhouettes and image features to follow recognizable points or planes. A body is easier to handle as several articulated roto shapes than as a single constantly changing outline. Sparse keyframes and stable spline handles reduce wobble. A one-point track supplies translation; two points can also indicate rotation and scale; four corners can warp an image onto a changing planar surface. Feature choice and search size determine whether any of these measurements is trustworthy. Tracks can also be inverted to stabilize a plate for repair and reapplied afterward. These are image-space methods: they should not be mistaken for a reconstruction of every depth in the shot.

That depth distinction leads to [[CG Integration and Render Passes]]. When camera motion reveals different sides or produces parallax between near and far features, a flat replacement breaks down. A three-dimensional camera solve uses many feature tracks to infer the filmed camera path, after rejecting moving or unreliable points and orienting the resulting scene. A rendered object can then occupy a plausible location, but its realism still depends on matching the plate's key and fill light, shadows, RGB densities, atmospheric falloff, and moving grain. An omnidirectional light reference or HDR image can provide the environmental color and intensity for CG, while radiosity computes diffuse light exchanged between surfaces. Light wrap should borrow blurred colors from the actual background at a constrained edge rather than apply a generic glow. Separating a render into lighting, surface, shadow, depth, and other passes retains control in compositing when one contribution needs revision.

Many shots do not need every pictured feature to become full geometry. [[Matte Painting and Scene Finishing]] exploits that economy. Painted or photographed cards staged at different depths can give a camera movement parallax, while simple geometry textured by a fixed projection camera can support a limited moving view. The farther the animation camera leaves the projection viewpoint, however, the more textures stretch and hidden regions are revealed; clean plates must fill those regions. Displacement maps can animate still water or raise terrain and city features from grayscale values. Scene finishing applies the same logic of constrained change to sky augmentation, day-for-night lighting, support-wire removal, atmospheric cards, and contact shadows: alter the needed region while retaining the rest of the original plate. Restoration and beauty work follow that principle at smaller scales: colorization keeps original tonal texture under roto-limited color, planar tracking moves a modified makeup patch, and selective skin smoothing restores grain after local blur. Time ramping changes the shot's temporal spacing but may require roto-limited optical-flow repair when invented frames smear overlapping motion.

Other effects begin with many interacting elements rather than a single replacement. [[Particle and Destruction VFX]] separates the birth and motion of particles from what is ultimately rendered at their positions. A point path may carry a cloud-image sprite for smoke, a real 3D model for debris, or an animated figure card for a crowd. Forces, lifetime, collision, and behavioral rules determine movement; cloth and fluid solvers address forms whose deformation and interaction exceed simple particle paths. A convincing explosion coordinates fractured structure, fire, smoke, ejected contents, and surviving damage in a timed sequence. A single fire clip pasted over a model omits the material relationships that make destruction legible. Likewise, a muzzle flash must briefly light nearby walls and the performer, while a cinematic bullet hit coordinates the timed foreground burst, exit material, and environmental spray.

Finally, [[Stereoscopic Imaging and Conversion]] asks how the completed scene is seen by two eyes. Separate left and right viewpoints create parallax, while their convergence establishes the depth perceived at the screen. Camera separation, parallel or inward-pointing rigs, and the chosen stereo window change the experience even when the underlying scene is unchanged. Anaglyph channels are one way to deliver the two views, not the source of their difference. A single-view shot can be converted by constructing an authored depth map, then using it to displace image pixels or a subdivided rubber-sheet surface before recording a second viewpoint. The map is an estimate, so its quality depends on extraction, roto, gradients, and the particular shot.

Taken together, these areas describe a sequence of questions: what is visible, how does it move, where does it sit in depth, how does it receive and reflect the scene's light, what other materials interact with it, and how will the audience view it? The techniques can be combined in different proportions. The most efficient solution is the least elaborate arrangement that keeps those relationships convincing across the actual frames and camera views the production needs.
