# Health Data Analysis

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Health Data Analysis is the chapter-level topic for turning health observations into reproducible descriptive and inferential evidence. Full Notes should use one of the focused child topics below rather than linking directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Health data analysis connects data management, statistical reasoning, software, and scientific judgment. A result is credible only when the path from the original records to the reported estimate can be inspected. That path begins with [[Statistical Computing Workflows]], where analysts choose an environment, organize code into ordered modules, manage packages, preserve metadata, and make every transformation reproducible. R and SAS embody different technical models, but both require a disciplined workflow: code should communicate intent, inputs should remain traceable, and an upstream correction should be able to flow through the entire analysis without manual reconstruction.

The working dataset is created through [[Analytic Data Preparation]]. Raw health files often contain many variables, special codes for unknown responses, inconsistent representations of dates, and records that do not belong to the target population. Analysts therefore inspect dimensions and variable classes, select the required columns, document inclusion and exclusion rules, recode categories, create indicator variables, and verify that partitioned records still account for the original data. Preparation is not clerical work preceding the “real” analysis. It defines the population, exposure, outcome, candidate confounders, missing-data treatment, and time window on which every later claim depends.

Numbers become easier to assess through [[Health Data Visualization]]. Histograms, box plots, quantile–quantile plots, and grouped displays reveal the shape and spread of continuous variables. Bar charts and stacked bars show how categorical levels are distributed, while scatter plots and correlation plots expose pairwise structure. Coefficient plots later help interpret fitted models. A useful graph is not decoration: its axes, encodings, labels, comparison groups, and scale should make the statistical question visible without implying more certainty than the data support.

Before fitting multivariable models, [[Descriptive Health Statistics]] establishes what the analytic sample contains. Summary statistics, quantiles, frequency and contingency tables, row and column proportions, and correlation matrices characterize the data. Bivariate tests then compare groups or relationships: chi-squared and Fisher tests address categorical associations, t-tests and one-way ANOVA compare means, and rank-based alternatives handle situations where parametric assumptions are doubtful. A journal-style Table 1 brings these results together so readers can see the sample, the outcome groups, and the candidate confounders before interpreting adjusted estimates.

Study design also depends on [[Statistical Power and Effect Size]]. Power calculations connect the magnitude of a scientifically meaningful effect with sample size, significance level, group allocation, and the intended statistical test. Effect sizes such as Cohen's d prevent planning from being reduced to a desired p-value. Sensitivity calculations across several plausible effects are often more informative than a single estimate, because the true effect is not known in advance and unequal outcome groups may impose practical limits.

The general reasoning used to build adjusted models belongs to [[Regression Model Development]]. Hypothesis-driven analysis prespecifies an exposure, outcome, and plausible confounders before model fitting, reducing the temptation to search indiscriminately for significance. Forward selection, backward elimination, and stepwise selection provide different paths through the candidate variables, but a manual process can keep categorical coding and scientific judgment visible. Model metadata records each iteration, while checks for collinearity, interaction, robustness, and relative fit help the analyst distinguish a defensible final model from a convenient one.

[[Linear Regression Analysis]] models a continuous outcome as an intercept plus contributions from explanatory variables. Its coefficients remain on the outcome's scale, which makes their interpretation direct, but residual behavior matters. Linearity, homoscedasticity, influential patterns, and the gap between raw and adjusted fit should be examined through residual plots and assumption tests. Adjusted R-squared and the regression F-statistic summarize aspects of fit, while confidence intervals show the uncertainty around individual estimates.

[[Logistic Regression Analysis]] addresses binary outcomes. Its fitted coefficients are log odds, but health research commonly exponentiates them to odds ratios with confidence intervals. The model introduces distinctive concerns: omitted-variable effects can change coefficient scale, separation can prevent ordinary maximum-likelihood convergence, and model deviance or information criteria may be needed to compare alternative specifications. Clear presentation identifies the reference group and makes it obvious whether an interval includes the no-association value of one.

[[Electronic Health Record Analytics]] begins where clinical work becomes data. Administrative systems, laboratory and vital-sign feeds, radiology, pharmacy, provider orders, and narrative documentation describe different parts of an encounter. Coding systems make those records comparable, but their categories reflect distinct purposes such as billing, observation, medication normalization, or image exchange. Electronic phenotyping therefore combines several forms of evidence and treats missingness, unit differences, duplicate records, and workflow-dependent documentation as analytic problems rather than assuming the record is a ready-made research table.

[[Biomedical Image Analysis]] turns pixels and voxels into localized, aligned, and measurable structures. Detection identifies candidate locations; segmentation estimates their boundaries; registration aligns images across time, patients, or modalities; and extracted features support classification or measurement. Simple thresholding can be effective when tissue intensities separate cleanly, while watershed or seeded region methods add spatial structure. Computer-aided diagnosis then places these operations inside a reading workflow, where false positives, presentation, and clinician acceptance matter as much as numerical sensitivity.

[[Clinical Sensor and Signal Analytics]] extends the evidence stream into continuously sampled physiology, motion, and context. Acquisition, preprocessing, transformation, modeling, and interpretation form a dependent pipeline: clock errors, detached sensors, missing values, and artifacts cannot be repaired merely by selecting a more complex learner. ECG processing illustrates the point. Power-line interference and motion artifacts must be controlled before QRS events or heart-rate patterns are trusted, and multichannel source separation still requires physiological interpretation of the recovered components.

[[Clinical and Biomedical Text Mining]] recovers computable meaning from reports and scientific prose. Morphological, lexical, syntactic, and semantic processing support extraction of concepts, codes, relations, and events. Clinical language adds difficult context: a finding may be negated, hypothetical, historical, or attributed to someone other than the patient. Biomedical literature adds specialized terminology and nested events. Corpora, annotation agreement, de-identification, and retained textual provenance are therefore central to evaluating whether an extracted structure is both accurate and safe to reuse.

[[Health Social Media Analytics]] observes health-related behavior outside formal care through search queries, public posts, and online communities. These streams can contribute early outbreak signals, patient experiences, lifestyle patterns, and possible adverse-event reports. Their speed and scale do not make them representative samples. News attention, platform demographics, automated content, willingness to disclose, and repeated posts can all alter the apparent signal, so digital indicators are best treated as complementary observations that require external epidemiological or clinical validation.

[[Healthcare Information Retrieval]] connects an information need with knowledge-based resources. Bibliographic records, full text, annotations, and aggregated evidence offer different levels of detail. Controlled terminologies bridge synonyms and organize concepts, while manual and automated indexing expose documents to search. Exact-match queries give explicit control and reproducibility; ranked partial matching tolerates vocabulary uncertainty. System metrics such as precision and recall must be paired with user-oriented evaluation because a retrieved document is valuable only if it supports the clinical, patient, or research decision at hand.

[[Clinical Prediction and Temporal Analytics]] models both outcomes and the order or duration of events. Prediction methods must address asymmetric error costs, calibration, censoring, and transport to new settings. Longitudinal mining adds sequences, interval relations, exposure-event windows, and disease trajectories. A recurring pattern can be frequent without being causal or clinically useful, especially when visit timing and documentation depend on illness. External validation and explicit temporal definitions keep the model tied to the decision and population it is meant to serve.

[[Health Data Privacy and Integration]] addresses two consequences of combining rich datasets. Clinical and genomic evidence can be joined before modeling, after separate models, or through intermediate representations, but high-dimensional feature discovery must remain inside validation. The same combinations that improve prediction can identify people through quasi-identifiers. Generalization, suppression, synthetic data, perturbation, and swapping each trade disclosure risk against analytic fidelity. Privacy and utility therefore have to be evaluated together for the intended release and analysis.

[[Pervasive Health and Clinical Decision Support]] carries analysis into daily life and the point of care. Body area networks and smart environments can observe activity, physiology, and changes from a personal baseline, while decision-support systems turn patient data into alerts or recommendations. The technical chain includes sensing, communication, inference, interface design, and an escalation pathway. A correct model can still fail through weak batteries, poor workflow timing, alert fatigue, unclear explanations, or inadequate regulation and oversight.

[[Healthcare Fraud Analytics]] looks for deceptive behavior at several levels: individual claims, episodes of care, provider profiles, and coordinated networks. Aggregation and peer comparison reveal patterns that a single transaction hides, while change-point and cumulative-sum methods detect shifts over time. These signals prioritize scarce investigative attention; they do not establish intent. Specialty, policy, patient complexity, and coding changes provide legitimate explanations for unusual behavior, so analytic evidence must be paired with auditable review.

[[Pharmaceutical Discovery Analytics]] spans target discovery and post-marketing safety. Chemical, biological, genomic, clinical, literature, and patient-generated sources can propose drug-target links, repurposing opportunities, and adverse-event signals. The evidence is systematically incomplete: known interactions and reported harms are recorded more often than true negatives or uneventful exposures. Network prediction, disproportionality analysis, and multivariate models are therefore hypothesis-generating tools whose conclusions depend on experimental, clinical, and cross-source corroboration.

[[Life Science Data Analysis with R]] extends this workflow into the mathematical and computational methods needed for high-throughput biology. It connects reproducible R data handling with sampling theory, exploratory graphics, matrix formulations of linear models, multiple-testing control, empirical Bayes estimation, dimension reduction, statistical learning, and correction of latent batch structure. These methods are especially valuable when each biological sample produces thousands of related measurements and the analyst must separate scientific signal from technical variation without losing reproducibility.

Finally, [[Survival and Time-to-Event Analysis]] adds time and censoring to outcome analysis. An event indicator must be paired with a valid time-to-event measure, and the observation window determines who is treated as having experienced the event. Kaplan–Meier estimates and conditional survival tables describe the changing risk set; log-rank or generalized Wilcoxon tests compare curves. Regression may assume a parametric distribution or use the semiparametric Cox proportional hazards model, but the distributional and proportional-hazards assumptions must be checked. If event times are poorly measured or selection removes most events, a simpler logistic analysis may be more honest than a sophisticated survival model.

Together, these topics form a single chain of evidence. Computing practices protect reproducibility; records, images, signals, text, and online observations define what can be measured; retrieval and visualization make evidence inspectable; privacy governs how it can be combined and shared; and predictive, temporal, operational, and pharmaceutical methods turn it into decisions or hypotheses. None of the later stages can compensate for a serious failure in acquisition, representation, validation, or workflow design. Health data analysis is therefore best understood as a documented reasoning process rather than a collection of isolated statistical commands.
