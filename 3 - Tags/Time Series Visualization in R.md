# Time Series Visualization in R

Time Series Visualization in R is the chapter-tier topic for representing indexed observations, comparing temporal variables, conditioning relationships on time, and supporting interactive or animated exploration.

## Overview Chapter

Time-series visualization begins before a line is drawn. A temporal observation has a value and a position in an ordered index, and the representation must preserve that relationship. [[R Time Series Data Structures]] provides this foundation through indexed objects whose observations can be separated from, but remain associated with, dates or other ordered coordinates. Calendar-aware monthly and quarterly indexes express the resolution actually present in the data. Windowing isolates meaningful intervals, aggregation moves to a coarser temporal grid, and missing-value operations make explicit whether gaps are removed, interpolated, or filled from earlier observations. These choices affect every later graphic because they determine which values appear, when they appear, and which apparent continuities come from processing rather than measurement.

Once an indexed series is ready, [[Annotated Time-Series Displays]] place time on the horizontal axis and use graphical context to explain what the line means. Separate panels can preserve the variation of variables with different units, while alternating period backgrounds reveal boundaries between years or other intervals. Threshold blocks identify times that meet an analytical condition, running means expose slower movement, and long-term averages establish a seasonal reference. Local extrema can be marked selectively, and calendar rasters can trade a continuous trajectory for a compact view of recurring periods. Panel functions make these layers reproducible by controlling how backgrounds, curves, points, references, and text are composed.

A single series is rarely the whole problem. [[Comparative Time-Series Displays]] addresses collections of variables that must be seen together without turning the figure into an opaque knot. Superposition works when series share a meaningful scale, with thin translucent lines revealing where trajectories accumulate. Banking adjusts aspect ratio so slopes remain perceptually useful, while cut-and-stack layouts preserve that ratio for long sequences. Small multiples separate paths but align their time coordinates. Horizon graphs compress signed deviations into colored bands, allowing many panels to fit vertically. Stacked areas, ThemeRivers, and streamgraphs instead show how additive components combine, although their moving baselines favor composition patterns over exact comparison.

Time can also shape a graphic without occupying an axis. [[Time-Conditioned Relationship Displays]] use periods as groups, facets, colors, or the order connecting observations. Scatterplot matrices compare every pair of variables, and temporal color can expose whether a relationship belongs to a particular season. Hexagonal bins summarize dense clouds. Conditioning divides the same relationship into comparable monthly, yearly, or statistical panels. Temporal polylines reveal direction through a two-variable space, whereas year panels trade continuous paths for easier period comparison. Variable-size points can introduce another quantity, and direct labels or carefully ordered colors help readers discriminate trajectories when ordinary legends become overloaded.

Static views establish structure, but [[Interactive and Animated Time-Series Graphics]] allow readers to request detail and follow change. Hover readouts recover exact values without filling the plot with labels. Zooming and shaded range selection move between overview and local inspection. Highlighting and interactive legends isolate a path while retaining the surrounding collection, and coordinated brushing connects the same records across multiple displays. Animation maps time to frames: bubbles can move, resize, and recolor as their observations change, with transitions helping preserve identity. A time slider is essential for locating the current frame and revisiting an interval, while a static path remains a valuable complement because motion is transient and difficult to compare from memory.

Together these topics form a sequence from representation to interpretation. The indexed object states what is observed and when; annotated plots establish temporal reference; comparative layouts reveal shared and divergent behavior; conditioned displays show how relationships change across periods; and interaction supplies details that would overcrowd a static page. No single display is universally best. A useful workflow selects the view according to the task, keeps scales and transformations visible, and combines complementary graphics when one encoding must sacrifice precision, continuity, or context to expose another kind of pattern.
