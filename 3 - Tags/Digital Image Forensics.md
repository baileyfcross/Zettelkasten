# Digital Image Forensics

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

Digital Image Forensics is the chapter-level topic for reasoning about photographic provenance, image formation, scene consistency, encoding history, duplicated or resampled content, sensor traces, and synthetic imagery. ## Overview Chapter

Digital image forensics asks whether the history claimed for a photograph is compatible with the evidence preserved inside it. A digital image is not produced in one step. Light first interacts with the scene, travels through the camera's optics, reaches a sensor, is converted into samples, passes through device-specific processing, and is finally encoded in a file. Editing, recompression, publication, and online sharing add further transformations. Each stage can leave regularities, and tampering may disturb them. The field therefore works backward from the available file, comparing its metadata, visible content, pixel statistics, and encoding artifacts with models of how an unaltered photograph should have formed.

[[Image Authenticity and Forensic Reasoning]] establishes how those tests should be interpreted. Authentication is not a mathematical proof that nothing happened to a file; it is a failure to find tampering with the suitable techniques that were applied. A positive result may reveal a specific edit, or it may show only that an image was resaved. A negative result is more informative when a high-quality, high-resolution file retains rich evidence than when heavy compression, resizing, or stripped metadata have destroyed it. [[Image Provenance]] can be investigated through reverse search and internal traces, but incomplete indexes and editable records make corroboration essential. Combining independent tests increases confidence, while explicit assumptions and the possibility of deliberate countermeasures keep the conclusion appropriately bounded.

[[Image Metadata and JPEG Signatures]] studies information around the pixels. EXIF directories may record the device, lens, exposure, time, position, software history, and an embedded thumbnail. An analyst can compare those claims with the visible scene and with one another. The JPEG file itself also records choices made by an encoder: quantization tables, thumbnail dimensions and processing, and marker-segment packaging may characterize a camera type or editing application. A contradiction can rule out a claimed origin, but a match cannot prove it because metadata can be rewritten and encoding conventions can be shared or deliberately copied.

[[Exposure and Lighting Forensics]] moves from file records to physical scene behavior. Aperture, shutter speed, and ISO jointly determine exposure while producing different depth-of-field, motion-blur, and noise effects. Their recorded values should be plausible for the photograph. Illumination adds further constraints. A camera flash creates characteristic falloff and direction, cast shadows imply projected light positions, planar reflections require a consistent mirror geometry, and lens flare can constrain the location of a bright source. These cues are powerful precisely because a pasted object must fit the lighting of the whole scene, yet multiple lights, unusual surfaces, and unknown motion mean that the appropriate physical model must be chosen carefully.

[[Photogrammetric Image Analysis]] uses perspective geometry to connect the two-dimensional image to possible three-dimensional scenes. The pinhole camera model explains apparent scale, focal length, field of view, and the fundamental ambiguity that every image point represents a ray of possible depths. Parallel lines define vanishing points, coplanar directions define vanishing lines, and three mutually perpendicular directions can locate the camera's principal point. The same projective structure supports planar rectification and more elaborate facial or scene reconstruction. Additional constraints such as known dimensions, body articulation, contact points, and surface orientation can expose a composite that would require an implausible camera, object size, or pose.

[[JPEG Compression Forensics]] treats file encoding as a historical trace. JPEG converts color into luminance and chrominance, divides channels into blocks, transforms those blocks into spatial frequencies, quantizes coefficients, and encodes the result. This ordinary process creates blocking and ringing that must not be confused with manipulation. It also makes prior saves detectable. Recompression at several candidate qualities can reveal a region with a different effective quality as a JPEG ghost. When two saves use different quantization steps, periodic holes and peaks may appear in transform-coefficient histograms. These findings demonstrate incompatible compression histories, though they do not by themselves explain why the image was resaved.

[[Copy-Move and Resampling Forensics]] addresses pixel operations used during editing. Copy-move forgery duplicates material within one photograph, often inheriting the source's color and noise well enough to blend naturally. A detector reduces an otherwise enormous search by finding salient corners, describing their local gradients, matching similar descriptors, and grouping matches that share a translation, rotation, or scale. Pixel-level verification then separates a coherent clone from ordinary repeated structures. Scaling or rotating any inserted region also requires interpolation. That resampling creates periodic local correlations which may remain measurable even when no visual artifact is obvious, although global resizing is a benign source of the same class of trace.

[[Camera Sensor and Synthetic Image Forensics]] examines evidence nearest to capture and evidence that may be absent when no camera captured the scene at all. A color filter array measures only one color at each sensor location, and demosaicing estimates the missing channels, producing periodic correlations that local editing can disrupt. Stable photo-response non-uniformity creates a fingerprint capable of linking an image to one physical sensor, while local noise differences can expose compositing or processing. Generated faces and scenes may omit these camera traces or biological signals such as subtle pulse-driven facial color changes. Such detectors remain part of a moving contest: once a missing regularity becomes known, synthesis systems can be trained to imitate it.

Taken together, the chapter's methods turn authenticity into a structured comparison between a claimed history and many independent consequences of that history. The strongest analysis begins with the least altered available file, selects tests whose assumptions fit its content and format, distinguishes ordinary processing from deceptive alteration, and reports both positive evidence and limitations. No technique makes photographs self-authenticating, but converging physical, geometric, statistical, and file-structural evidence can make an unsupported story increasingly difficult to sustain.
