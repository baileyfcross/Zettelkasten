# Cloud Computing

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

Cloud Computing is the chapter-level topic for shared computing resources delivered as services, the models used to package and combine those services, their economics and security, and the organizational practices required to adopt them. Full Notes should link to a focused child topic rather than directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Cloud computing is a way of delivering configurable computing capabilities as services rather than tying each workload permanently to equipment owned and managed by its consumer. The change is both technical and organizational. Shared resource pools improve utilization, automation makes capacity available on demand, network access separates use from physical location, and measurement connects consumption to management and price. Those properties create flexibility, but they also move important boundaries: the consumer gains less direct responsibility for infrastructure while becoming more dependent on service contracts, interfaces, providers, networks, and the rules governing remote data.

### From virtual resources to cloud services

[[Cloud Computing Foundations]] distinguishes the cloud model from ordinary hosting or virtualization. Server virtualization allows several virtual machines to share one physical host, while application virtualization lets many users reach centrally hosted software. Both can reduce unused capacity and endpoint management. Neither automatically provides cloud computing. A true cloud service also needs broad network access, on-demand self-service, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured use. Portals, orchestration, security, reporting, demand management, and billing turn virtual resources into a consumable service.

Elasticity is a particularly important relationship. Horizontal scaling changes the number of comparable resource instances; vertical scaling changes the capacity of an individual instance. A cloud scales out to meet greater demand and scales in when the demand recedes, using a shared pool so released resources can serve other consumers. Multi-tenancy spreads a service across several consumers, increasing efficiency while requiring isolation among their activity and data. Measurement makes this movement visible and supplies a basis for both capacity decisions and usage-oriented charging.

A service is judged by the value it delivers rather than by every internal machine used to deliver it. A service level agreement defines the scope, quality, availability, and timeliness promised to the consumer. Service level objectives translate parts of that promise into measurable targets such as response time, throughput, or recovery. An operational level agreement applies the same idea between internal supplier and consumer groups. These commitments keep abstraction accountable: the consumer may not operate the infrastructure, but it still needs evidence that the service satisfies its purpose.

### Abstraction and deployment choices

[[Cloud Service and Deployment Models]] supplies two independent dimensions for describing a service. The service model identifies its abstraction level. Infrastructure as a service supplies servers, storage, and networking while leaving substantial system control to the consumer. Platform as a service adds an operating environment, runtime, middleware, and development facilities. Software as a service exposes a complete hosted application. Information as a service transforms raw data into information relevant to a situation, and business process as a service coordinates applications, information, people, and workflow to deliver a business outcome.

Higher abstraction reduces the implementation detail managed by the consumer, but it also places more behavior inside the provider's boundary. A database service can become a reusable platform capability; cloud storage provides remotely accessible durable capacity; cloud web hosting follows variable traffic with elastic resources; and cloud development or test environments can be provisioned without purchasing their peak hardware. These uses are related by abstraction, yet their requirements differ. A developer may value runtime control and portability, while the consumer of a payroll process may care primarily about correct records, timely completion, and the interfaces to human-resources data.

The deployment model identifies who can consume the service and the boundary through which it is delivered. A public cloud serves a broad population, normally across the Internet. A private cloud serves one person or organization. A community cloud serves entities with a shared industry, geography, regulation, or interest. A hybrid cloud combines component clouds and may keep sensitive work private while using public capacity or standard services elsewhere. Cloud bursting extends work into another cloud during excess demand, but it succeeds only when data, control, security, and service behavior can cross that boundary.

### Composing and buying capabilities

[[Cloud Service Composition and Brokerage]] treats cloud systems as combinations of reusable services rather than isolated platforms. A cloud cell performs one fundamental function, such as storage, databases, web serving, messaging, or firewalling. Comparable cells can be aggregated to share load or provide continuity; distinct cells can be composed into a larger capability. A mother cloud encloses the resulting set, optionally using a controller to orchestrate it. A cloud gear is a specialist cell providing a supporting function such as backup or encryption.

Standardization makes these building blocks reusable. A cloud service pattern names a recurring problem, identifies the component cells and their relationships, defines interfaces, and records consequences such as price and value. Changing a standard cell simplifies consistent maintenance across replicas, but every composite service using it must be retested. Federation takes composition across provider boundaries, letting internal and external services form one offering. Its speed and flexibility depend on interoperability rather than on the mere availability of several vendors.

The service supply chain has distinct actors. A creator builds and optimizes a capability, a provider orchestrates and sells it, and a consumer selects and uses it. A broker can reduce direct dependency on one provider, combine several services, or choose among them according to cost and service quality. A service integrator coordinates the ongoing portfolio, while a service catalog records approved offerings, prices, agreements, and suppliers. The book also proposes a cloud service exchange in which standardized, interoperable capabilities could be discovered and traded as commodities. That 2016-era proposal makes the architectural requirement clear: real substitution requires compatible interfaces and commitments, not just comparable labels.

### Relating price to business value

[[Cloud Pricing Models]] explains how provider costs become consumer charges. A cost model includes equipment, software, labor, electricity, cooling, space, capital, depreciation, inflation, risk, and eventual technology refresh. A price model adds business strategy and margin, then expresses the result through a unit the market can buy. Consumption pricing meters infrastructure resources; transaction pricing counts discrete units of work; subscriptions sell access for a period. Fixed, volume, and tiered arrangements emphasize service scope, quantity, or differentiated commitments.

Performance-oriented pricing connects payment to results. Outcome pricing rewards achievement of an agreed benefit, business-linked pricing uses a business performance indicator, and gain-share pricing gives the provider part of an improvement beyond a baseline. Marketing-oriented approaches include freemium offerings and a razor-and-blades relationship in which an inexpensive device or base component leads to recurring service charges. Hybrid pricing combines mechanisms to balance predictability, affordability, risk, and incentive. Every model depends on precise measurement: ambiguity about allocated, average, peak, or completed units becomes ambiguity about both cost and responsibility.

[[Cloud Business Value and Finance]] considers the other side of the comparison. Converting capital expenditure into operating expenditure can preserve funds that would otherwise be tied up in equipment sized for an uncertain peak. Elasticity can reduce idle capacity and avoid lost opportunity when demand exceeds owned infrastructure. Location flexibility expands access, time-to-market agility removes procurement delay, price flexibility lets the purchasing model change with volume, and asset optimization keeps infrastructure life cycles from outlasting the products they support.

These benefits need financial evidence. Total cost of ownership includes migration, integration, support, maintenance, backup, network, dependency, and exit costs in addition to the advertised price. Payback period estimates when accumulated benefit recovers the investment. Return on investment compares gain with cost, while net present value discounts future cash flows to a common present time. The experience curve suggests that cost and effort can decline as providers and consumers accumulate experience, but neither scale nor cloud branding guarantees value. A fair decision compares the complete costs and benefits under consistent time, demand, and risk assumptions.

### Protecting information and preserving service

[[Cloud Security and Data Protection]] begins with the properties information must retain. The CIA triad covers confidentiality, integrity, and availability. The Parkerian hexad adds possession or control, authenticity, and utility, distinguishing cases in which data remains secret but is held by the wrong party, is correctly preserved but falsely attributed, or is available in an unusable form. A security container groups systems, users, storage, software, backups, and channels that share a policy, with identification, authentication, authorization, segmentation, and controlled crossings enforcing its boundary.

Protection must follow data through rest, motion, and active use. Data loss prevention coordinates discovery, monitoring, encryption, access, retention, and destruction across that full life cycle. Personally identifiable information requires special attention because isolated facts may become identifying when combined. Privacy asks whether data should be collected or used, not merely whether an attacker can read it. Sovereignty and jurisdiction ask who owns the data and which laws apply when users, providers, processors, and storage occupy different countries. Cloud security is shared: a provider may protect the platform, while the consumer remains responsible for application behavior, credentials, configuration, and lawful use.

[[Cloud Security Monitoring and Resilience]] joins preventive controls with evidence and recovery. Firewalls restrict network crossings, endpoint protection reduces compromise at the user's device, and encrypted channels protect information in transit. Certificates help authenticate endpoints, checksums reveal changes under their stated trust assumptions, and access controls restrict queue, application, and data operations. Intrusion detection examines logs for suspicious behavior; prevention systems can block it. Fuzzing and penetration testing deliberately search for weaknesses in a controlled setting.

Some failures will still occur. Threat assessment and risk analysis guide priorities before an incident, while security-event monitoring helps identify what is happening. A business continuity plan states how essential work will continue, and disaster recovery restores the technical environment. Recovery time objective measures how long service can remain unavailable; recovery point objective measures how much recent data may be lost. Secondary providers and tested failover can support those goals, but a backup or alternate contract is not continuity until the organization proves that people, data, credentials, and applications can use it.

### Integration, adoption, and durable recovery

[[Cloud Integration and Messaging]] provides the contracts that let independently operated components cooperate. Interoperability includes governance, security, data, processes, monitoring, billing, and reporting as well as protocols. Data integration needs a common format and a common data model. Process integration defines how one workflow triggers or substitutes for another. A service bus can mediate transports and transformations, but it cannot manufacture shared business meaning.

Middleware supports data-, object-, or message-oriented interaction. Message queues decouple sender and receiver in time and technology. Synchronous messaging waits for a response; asynchronous messaging permits later processing. Reliable, guaranteed, and transacted delivery express different completion promises. Priority changes retrieval order, correlation identifiers join related messages, point-to-point delivery targets a receiver, and broadcast delivery reaches interested subscribers. Each feature trades simplicity for a more specific contract about loss, duplication, ordering, and completion.

[[Cloud Governance and Adoption]] connects those technical relationships to organizational change. An adoption strategy compares the as-is landscape with a to-be state and turns the gap into sequenced work. A single-provider model offers clear centralized control; a multi-provider model adds choice but requires integration; a third-party integrator can coordinate the portfolio. Service-delivery and service-consumption models give business units progressively more direct responsibility while central technology leadership shifts toward catalogs, standards, governance, and audit.

Requirements should cover features, maturity, interoperability, security, availability, resilience, network capacity, price, risk, continuity, support, billing, and reporting. A weighted decision matrix makes priorities and evidence visible during vendor evaluation. Critical success factors turn essential requirements into owned measures. A cloud maturity model then tracks improvement across people, processes, monitoring, oversight, and financial management. This governance must also address shadow IT and vendor lock-in: easy purchasing creates local agility, but unmanaged services and difficult exits can turn short-term convenience into long-term risk.

[[Backup and Recovery Strategies]] focuses specifically on recoverable data. A fileset defines the scope, and each run creates a backup set. Full backups copy everything; differential backups copy changes since the last full set; incremental backups copy changes since the previous increment. These choices trade backup speed and space against restoration complexity. Archives have a different purpose: they retain infrequently used information over long periods, whereas backups restore changing operational data quickly. Rotation schemes such as first-in-first-out and grandfather-father-son organize recovery points, and offsite or multi-provider copies reduce dependence on one location. Whatever the scheme, restoration tests are the decisive evidence.

### Processes, work, and social reach

[[Cloud Business Process Automation]] raises abstraction from technology to recurring work. Business process management designs, enacts, measures, and improves workflows. Reengineering first asks whether a process or step is necessary, then decides which parts should use software, information services, complete cloud processes, human judgment, or non-cloud systems. Vertical processes participate directly in an organization's distinctive product; horizontal processes such as payroll and training recur across industries and are easier to standardize.

A composite business process can join current information, hosted applications, infrastructure, and manual decisions. Control interfaces signal when work should occur, while information interfaces supply the data needed to perform it. Tax planning, payroll, training, examinations, and health monitoring illustrate different arrangements. Their value comes from consistency, repeatability, scalability, and per-use economics, but only when the complete workflow—including its organizational inputs and exceptions—is understood.

Finally, [[Cloud-Enabled Work and Society]] examines where these services are used. Thin and zero clients move applications and data away from locally managed endpoints. Bring-your-own-device programs accept diverse personal hardware, while ubiquitous computing extends access across device, place, and time. Cloud office tools and collaboration services support distributed work, and the IT department can evolve from an owner of centralized systems into a broker of approved capabilities or a standards function distributed through business units.

Personal and societal clouds organize services around a person or community. A cloud of things connects devices to monitoring and control, while an Internet of Services supplies the surrounding maintenance, billing, and workflow. The book's proposed cloud of things and services combines both, with examples involving homes, wearables, cities, utilities, and retail. Personal health clouds show the promise and the risk: measurement and alerts can improve timely attention, while intimate data creates strong privacy and ownership obligations. Cloud automation can expand productivity and business reach, but it can also concentrate data, shift work, and deepen provider dependency.

Across all of these topics, cloud computing is best understood as a system of negotiated boundaries. Abstraction decides what the consumer manages; deployment decides who shares the service; composition decides how capabilities fit together; pricing and finance decide how value and risk are exchanged; security decides who can trust data and operation; integration decides how independent services cooperate; and governance decides who remains accountable. The technology is useful when those boundaries are explicit, measurable, interoperable, and recoverable—not simply when computing happens somewhere else.
