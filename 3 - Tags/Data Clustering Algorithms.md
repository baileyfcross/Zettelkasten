# Data Clustering Algorithms

Parent topic: [[Cluster Analysis and Data Mining]]

Data Clustering Algorithms is the chapter-level topic for modern methods that discover groups across high-dimensional, streaming, relational, uncertain, multimedia, and constrained data. Full Notes link to focused child topics rather than directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Clustering is not one algorithm but a family of ways to define and recover structure. [[Cluster Analysis Foundations]] supplies the common vocabulary: objects are represented by features, a proximity or probabilistic model expresses resemblance, and an objective or structural criterion determines what counts as a useful group. That framing matters because the same records can support several legitimate clusterings. A method that seeks compact spherical groups answers a different question from one that seeks dense regions, graph communities, correlated subspaces, or alternate views. Modern clustering therefore begins by matching the representation and algorithm to the geometry, scale, and intended interpretation of the data.

[[Unsupervised Feature Selection for Clustering]] moves feature choice inside that unsupervised problem. Without class labels, relevance must be inferred from neighborhood preservation, spectral structure, sparsity, or the quality of clusters induced by a feature subset. Filter methods score features independently of a final clusterer, wrapper methods evaluate subsets through clustering performance, and hybrid methods combine both. Feature weighting can also let a clustering procedure suppress irrelevant dimensions while it estimates groups. These methods are especially important because noise dimensions can overwhelm meaningful proximity before an algorithm ever begins grouping points.

Two classical algorithm families illustrate how different structural assumptions lead to different outputs. [[Partition and K-Means Clustering]] assigns every object to one of a fixed number of groups and typically optimizes dispersion around representative centers. It is efficient and scalable, but sensitive to initialization, outliers, and non-spherical structure. [[Hierarchical Clustering Methods]] instead builds nested partitions by repeated merges or splits. Linkage rules determine which geometry the hierarchy favors, and the dendrogram exposes solutions at several resolutions. Neither family is universally preferable: partitioning emphasizes a single economical summary, while hierarchy emphasizes relationships among possible resolutions.

[[Density and Grid-Based Clustering]] avoids requiring globular groups. Density methods grow clusters through connected neighborhoods of sufficiently dense points, leaving sparse observations as noise. OPTICS records a reachability ordering that reveals structure across density scales, while DENCLUE describes density through influence functions and attractors. Grid methods quantize the data space and operate on populated cells rather than individual records. STING stores statistical summaries, WaveCluster transforms the grid to expose dense regions, and CLIQUE searches dense subspaces. These techniques can recover irregular shapes and reduce data-access costs, although results still depend on resolution and density parameters.

[[Outlier Detection and DBSCAN]] connects this density view to anomaly handling. DBSCAN distinguishes core, border, and noise points through neighborhood support, while local-density comparisons identify observations that are sparse relative to their surroundings. The same mechanisms that outline a cluster therefore also explain why some observations remain outside it.

[[Model-Based Clustering]] treats membership as inference under a data-generating model. Finite mixtures associate clusters with probability components, and expectation-maximization alternates between estimating membership probabilities and component parameters. This makes overlap, uncertainty, and covariance structure explicit, but model misspecification and local optima remain serious concerns. [[Categorical Data Clustering]] handles records for which ordinary geometric means are meaningless. Algorithms such as ROCK, STIRR, CACTUS, and CLICK replace Euclidean geometry with neighbor links, relational reinforcement, co-occurrence summaries, or graph structures derived from categorical values.

Linear-algebraic methods provide another view. [[Matrix Factorization and Spectral Clustering]] represents observations through latent nonnegative factors or eigenvectors of a similarity graph. Nonnegative matrix factorization creates additive, parts-based representations that can support both clustering and co-clustering. Spectral methods embed graph relationships in a low-dimensional eigenspace before applying a simpler partitioner. Ratio-cut and normalized-cut relaxations clarify which graph separation objective is being approximated. Their flexibility depends on a well-chosen affinity graph and on computational methods capable of extracting the needed factors or eigenvectors.

[[High-Dimensional and Subspace Clustering]] addresses the fact that distance loses contrast when many irrelevant dimensions accumulate. A group may be visible in only a subset of attributes, and different groups may occupy different subspaces. Axis-parallel methods search selected dimensions, projected methods associate each cluster with its own feature set, and correlation methods allow arbitrarily oriented manifolds. CLIQUE exploits monotonicity of dense regions, while PROCLUS and ORCLUS combine partitioning with subspace estimation. The search space is enormous, so practical methods balance coverage, redundancy, interpretability, and computational cost.

Scale and motion change the computational problem again. [[Stream and Big Data Clustering]] must summarize observations that arrive continuously or exceed one machine's memory. STREAM compresses batches into weighted representatives; BIRCH uses compact clustering-feature summaries; CluStream stores microclusters and a pyramidal history for later macroclustering. DenStream and D-Stream adapt density ideas to evolving data, and HPStream combines fading summaries with projected dimensions. Distributed approaches partition work across processors or MapReduce stages, but a scalable implementation must still preserve enough statistics to support a meaningful global solution.

Specialized data types require specialized representations. [[Document and Multimedia Clustering]] works with sparse word vectors, snippets, microblogs, images, faces, and video stories. Text methods must handle extreme sparsity, document length, and background knowledge; multimedia methods must bridge low-level features and semantic concepts. Visual words, temporal segmentation, and multimodal fusion turn raw signals into objects that clustering can compare. [[Time-Series and Biological Clustering]] must additionally respect order, alignment, correlation, and domain structure. Dynamic time warping aligns sequences with differing pace, trajectory methods compare paths, and biclustering or triclustering finds coherent gene-expression patterns across subsets of genes, conditions, and time.

Relations among objects can be primary data rather than a side effect of feature construction. [[Network and Uncertain Data Clustering]] includes graph bisection, multilevel partitioning, Markov flow, hypergraph models, overlapping communities, and clustering when each value is a distribution or set of possible worlds. Network objectives balance cut size against community cohesion. Uncertain-data methods propagate uncertainty into distances, density reachability, centroids, or expected objectives instead of collapsing every object to a single point. Both cases warn against forcing relational or probabilistic information into an ordinary flat table too early.

Human knowledge can guide the search without fully supervising it. [[Interactive and Semi-Supervised Clustering]] allows analysts to steer a visualization, choose among nearby solutions, provide seed labels, or state must-link and cannot-link constraints. Graph cuts, label propagation, and learned embeddings can spread limited guidance through the data. Interaction is useful when quality includes domain meaning that no internal index captures, but it also makes provenance important: the final grouping should record which constraints and interventions shaped it.

A dataset may also contain several defensible organizations. [[Alternative and Ensemble Clustering]] either seeks a substantially different view from an existing solution or combines many candidate partitions into a consensus. Alternative clustering can use explicit constraints, orthogonality, or information-theoretic objectives to avoid rediscovering the same structure. Ensemble methods work from co-association, voting, probabilistic combination, or consensus optimization. Diversity among inputs is valuable only when the component solutions retain useful signal; otherwise an ensemble can merely stabilize shared error.

[[C++ Clustering Software Design]] connects these mathematical families to reusable implementation. It models datasets, distances, clusters, arguments, algorithm lifecycles, and results as cooperating C++ abstractions, then uses design patterns to keep shared control flow separate from method-specific computation. Its implementations of hierarchical, partitional, fuzzy, model-based, genetic, subspace, and parallel algorithms show how software architecture can make experiments consistent without erasing the distinctive mathematics of each method.

Finally, [[Cluster Validity and Simulation]] determines whether the output deserves interpretation. External measures compare clusters with known labels, internal measures balance cohesion and separation, and relative measures compare algorithms or parameter choices. Pair-counting, information-theoretic, silhouette, stability, and Monte Carlo procedures illuminate different failure modes. No single score can certify that a clustering is true. Validation must connect the algorithm's assumptions, the chosen representation, perturbation behavior, and the application purpose. Together, these topics form a disciplined workflow: select informative structure, choose an algorithm whose biases fit it, preserve uncertainty and scale where necessary, and test whether the discovered organization remains useful under credible alternatives.
