# Spatial Data Visualization in R

Spatial Data Visualization in R is the chapter-tier topic for representing geographic objects and fields through thematic, reference, physical, and interactive maps.

## Overview Chapter

A spatial display is built on decisions about what kind of geographic phenomenon is being represented. [[R Spatial Data Structures]] separates discrete objects from continuous fields. Points, lines, and polygons describe features with identifiable locations or boundaries, while rasters divide an extent into cells to approximate quantities or categories distributed across space. Simple-feature tables connect each geometry to ordinary attributes, and multilayer raster structures align variables or times over the same grid. None of these coordinates are self-explanatory: a coordinate reference system states how they relate to the Earth, and its projection determines which geometric properties a flat map preserves or distorts.

Point observations lead naturally to [[Proportional Symbol Mapping]]. A proportional symbol map places an area-scaled mark at each location so magnitude and geography remain visible together. Because circle area rather than radius must be proportional to the value, mathematical scaling matters, and perceptual correction may be used cautiously when area differences are systematically underestimated. Graduated symbols instead group values into a small number of classes, allowing range grading to choose reliably distinct sizes. Fisher-Jenks breaks can follow clusters in the distribution, while a well-constructed legend exposes the mapping from numbers to sizes. Labels identify selected places, and interpolation can transform discrete samples into a continuous surface, although that analytical model requires validation beyond the appearance of the resulting map.

Regional attributes are organized by [[Choropleth Mapping]]. A choropleth fills each polygon and thereby treats its value as applying throughout the region, which makes normalized rates or proportions generally more defensible than raw totals. Sequential palettes express ordered magnitude, while qualitative palettes distinguish categories without implying rank. Classed maps simplify values into intervals, forcing choices about class count and boundaries. Quantiles allocate similar numbers of regions to each color; natural breaks instead follow the observed distribution. Unclassed maps preserve a continuous scale but exceed the eye's ability to recover fine numeric differences. Small multiples support comparison across times or variables, and bivariate choropleths combine two attributes only when a restrained two-dimensional legend remains readable.

Continuous and directional phenomena require the encodings collected in [[Raster and Vector Field Mapping]]. Quantitative rasters map each cell through an ordered or diverging palette, with row and column summaries providing reduced views of broad spatial gradients. Diverging colors need a meaningful neutral reference, not merely a wide numeric range. Categorical rasters decode integer values through attribute tables and use qualitative colors, while bivariate rasters combine category and magnitude at the cost of additional visual complexity. Hill shading derives apparent relief from elevation and can supply physical context beneath a translucent theme. Vector fields add direction: arrows show local magnitude and orientation at samples, whereas streamlines reveal the continuous structure suggested by the field. Neither display should be so dense that its flow becomes texture.

Maps also need recognizable settings and usable delivery mechanisms. [[Reference and Interactive Mapping]] composes terrain, water, roads, boundaries, labels, and thematic layers through a deliberate visual hierarchy. Reprojection aligns layers, including transformation to Web Mercator when online reference tiles are used, but the destination projection's distortions remain part of the result. Interactive maps add pan, zoom, selection, tooltips, and popup graphics so dense attributes can be requested rather than permanently drawn. Synchronized views compare related layers without merging them into one crowded canvas. GeoJSON and KML move features between systems, but their geometry, projection, and attribute assumptions must be checked instead of treating file conversion as neutral.

These five areas describe a complete mapping argument. Data structures establish the geographic units and coordinate meaning. Symbol maps preserve point locations while encoding amount. Choropleths summarize attributes over regions. Raster and vector-field displays represent continuous magnitude, category, and direction. Reference and interactive layers then help readers locate features and inspect details. The visual design must remain accountable to the represented phenomenon: interpolation is not observation, a regional shade is not within-region variation, a projection is not the Earth, and interaction does not rescue an unclear initial map. Combining techniques is useful when their roles remain distinct and every scale, class, and transformation is visible to the reader.
