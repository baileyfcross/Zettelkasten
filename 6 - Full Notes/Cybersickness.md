# Cybersickness

Cybersickness is discomfort associated with virtual or three-dimensional systems and is symptomatically similar to motion sickness. It is also called simulator sickness or VR sickness in particular contexts.

A leading explanation is conflict among sensory signals. For example, a [[Visual Display]] may indicate movement while the vestibular system indicates that the body is stationary. Poor [[Feedback Compliance]], [[Latency]], low update rates, or misregistration can intensify the mismatch.

Symptoms can include headache, blurred vision, dizziness, disorientation, and nausea. Because susceptibility differs among people, evaluation should measure user comfort and provide rest or stopping procedures rather than relying only on system performance.

The broader term [[Sensory Conflict Theory]] describes the mismatch among visual, vestibular, and proprioceptive evidence, while [[Postural Instability Theory]] treats prolonged loss of stable posture as an alternative explanation. Display factors such as incorrect [[Interpupillary Distance]], a very wide [[Field of View]], optical distortion, flicker, and high pixel persistence can alter susceptibility. Symptoms can also outlast the session as a [[Simulator Aftereffect]], so a comfort check should extend beyond the moment the display is removed.

Visually induced self-motion, or vection, grows with the optic flow that signals movement through the scene. Continuous artificial acceleration can therefore create stronger conflict than a discrete relocation. A cockpit or temporary grid can reduce the moving portion of the view and give the user a stable frame of reference, while snap turning removes rotational acceleration. Susceptibility varies widely, so a locomotion system should expose comfort choices rather than assuming one method is safe for everyone.
