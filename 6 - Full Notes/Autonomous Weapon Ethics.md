# Autonomous Weapon Ethics

Autonomous weapon ethics concerns systems that can select or attack targets with reduced immediate human control. The central questions include whether lethal judgment may be delegated, how distinction and proportionality can be maintained, and who is accountable when an autonomous action causes wrongful harm.

The high stakes make technical reliability only one part of the issue. A machine may execute constraints without bearing [[Machine Moral Agency]], leaving responsibility distributed among military authorities, designers, operators, and states. Proposals to prohibit such weapons reflect the view that some decisions should remain human even if automation is operationally effective.

Control can place a person in the decision loop for every attack, on the loop with an opportunity to override, or outside the loop after deployment. None of these arrangements guarantees sound judgment: a supervisor may have too little time, defer to an automated classification, or act on incomplete sensor evidence. The architecture must therefore be evaluated together with targeting rules, communications, accountability, escalation risk, and the system's ability to recognize surrender or distinguish civilians.
