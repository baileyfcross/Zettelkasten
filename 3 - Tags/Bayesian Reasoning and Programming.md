# Bayesian Reasoning and Programming

Parent topics: [[Mathematics]] · [[Computer Science]]

Bayesian Reasoning and Programming is the chapter-level topic for representing incomplete knowledge with probability, assembling probabilistic models as executable programs, and answering questions through exact or approximate inference. Full Notes should use one of the focused child topics below rather than linking directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

Real systems cannot include every variable that affects the phenomenon they model. [[Probability Interpretation and Uncertainty]] begins with this gap between a model and its subject. Hidden influences make exact prediction impossible even when the known rules are applied correctly. The Bayesian view treats probability as a representation of a reasoner's state of knowledge rather than as an unexplained random substance in the world. Preliminary knowledge plays a role similar to axioms in logic: correct inference can still reach a poor conclusion when its assumptions are wrong, and observed disagreement creates an opportunity to revise those assumptions.

[[Probability Foundations]] supplies the calculus for that reasoning. A probability distribution assigns normalized plausibility across mutually exclusive and exhaustive alternatives. Conditional probability represents knowledge after information is imposed, while the conjunction rule relates joint and conditional distributions and yields Bayes theorem. Marginalization removes variables that are not part of the question. Prior probability records knowledge before selected evidence, likelihood expresses how compatible evidence is with a hypothesis, and the normalized posterior records the updated result. Conditional independence is the decisive modeling tool that lets a large joint distribution be decomposed without claiming that variables are independent in every context.

The methodology is made explicit in [[Bayesian Program Structure]]. A Bayesian program consists of a description and a question. The description combines a specification supplied by the modeler with identification from data. The specification names the variables, decomposes their joint distribution, and assigns a parametric form or another program to each factor. The question partitions variables into searched values, known evidence, and free variables that must be marginalized. This structure separates what is assumed, what is learned, and what is requested, allowing the same description to answer prediction, diagnosis, control, or classification questions.

Several uncertain observations can be combined through [[Bayesian Information Fusion]]. A simple model assumes measurements are conditionally independent once the hidden state is known, multiplying their individual support for each state. More realistic decompositions retain dependencies, ancillary clues, false alarms, or conflicts. A coherence variable can express whether two values should agree and can turn uncertain evidence into another probabilistic factor instead of forcing a hard observation. Fusion therefore means constructing one joint description that represents both the information sources and the conditions under which they can be trusted.

Large descriptions become reusable through [[Probabilistic Model Composition]]. A distribution in one decomposition can be supplied by asking another Bayesian program a question, creating a subroutine and enabling hierarchical models. A hidden choice variable can select among models or mix their predictions according to its own probability distribution. Conditional statements differ from deterministic branches because an uncertain choice causes all branches to contribute in proportion to their probability. Iteration builds repeated structures such as time-indexed state models, while superposition and coherent variable connections allow separate descriptions to participate in one inference.

Time-dependent reasoning belongs to [[Recursive Bayesian Estimation]]. A state-transition model predicts how a hidden state changes, and an observation model relates that state to measurements. Filtering estimates the present state from observations so far; prediction asks about a future state; smoothing revises a past state using later evidence. The posterior at one step becomes the prior for the next, keeping the history summarized recursively. Hidden Markov models, Kalman filters, particle filters, and Markov localization are specialized instances built from different state spaces, distributions, and inference methods.

The requested distribution is rarely obtained by writing Bayes theorem once. [[Bayesian Inference Algorithms]] first simplify the algebra implied by the decomposition and then evaluate the remaining sums, integrals, or optimizations. Variable elimination can reduce repeated computation, but exact inference becomes expensive in densely connected or high-dimensional models. Approximate methods draw samples or restrict the computation. Forward, importance, and rejection sampling use different proposal mechanisms, while Gibbs and Metropolis methods construct Markov chains. Monte Carlo integration estimates expectations from samples, and maximum-a-posteriori questions search for the most probable assignment rather than retaining the whole distribution.

Data contributes to a description through [[Bayesian Parameter and Structure Learning]]. Parameter estimation begins with a chosen family of distributions and learns its parameters from observations. Maximum likelihood retains the parameter value that best explains the data, while a Bayesian estimator keeps a distribution over parameter values and combines it with a prior. Conjugate priors make repeated updates analytically convenient. Expectation-maximization alternates between estimating latent values and improving parameters for incomplete-data problems. Structure learning searches among decompositions, using tree constraints or scores that reward fit while penalizing unnecessary complexity.

These methods become concrete in [[Probabilistic Robotics and Decision Models]]. Forward simulation predicts observations from proposed inputs and states; diagnosis reverses the question to estimate hidden causes from observed effects; probabilistic control selects actions while acknowledging uncertainty in the system. Classification, pattern recognition, behavior recognition, and sequence recognition all infer hidden categories or models from evidence. A Markov decision process adds actions and reward to state transitions, while partial observability requires decisions over a distribution of possible states rather than one assumed state.

Together, the topics form a disciplined workflow. The modeler accepts incompleteness, chooses variables, states conditional structure, identifies unknown parameters, and asks an explicit question. Inference then applies the probability rules to that description, exactly when feasible and approximately when necessary. The resulting answer is conditional on the model and evidence rather than a declaration of certainty. Bayesian programming makes this dependency visible enough that assumptions, learned information, computational approximations, and decisions can be inspected and revised.
