# Computing History

Parent topic: [[Computer Science]]

The history of computing is not a straight succession of faster calculators. Modern computing emerged when calculation, coded information, automatic control, electronics, communication, and human interaction converged around programmable digital machines. Its development was shaped jointly by technical possibility, military and scientific demand, commercial strategy, user communities, standards, and design.

## Overview Chapter

### The digital paradigm joins older information technologies

[[Digital Information and Early Data Processing]] begins before the electronic computer. Counting boards and mechanical calculators progressively mechanized arithmetic, especially the difficult carry from one decimal position to the next. Punched-card systems then separated information from the machinery that processed it. Herman Hollerith encoded census records as holes, and installations combined key punches, sorters, tabulators, and calculators into flexible data-processing workflows. People supplied the program by carrying decks, setting switches, and deciding which machine should act next.

Punched cards also carried control. Jacquard looms selected threads from encoded patterns, and Charles Babbage planned to use cards to direct a general-purpose Analytical Engine. The same physical medium could therefore represent data or determine action. Telegraphy added long-distance coded communication: Morse pulses anticipated digital signaling, while later information theory formalized the relationship between symbols, codes, and reliable transmission.

The digital paradigm unified these streams by representing calculation, logic, control, and communication through discrete states. Analog devices modeled quantities with continuous physical relationships, while digital systems manipulated codes. The boundary was initially fluid, but binary electronics made representations easy to copy, store, and transform reliably. A computer became a convergence of calculation, storage, control, input-output, and communication rather than an isolated arithmetic mechanism.

### War accelerates machines and exposes their limits

[[Wartime Computing and Early Electronic Machines]] follows several independent paths that converged between 1935 and 1945. Konrad Zuse built program-controlled binary machines and recognized that one general mechanism could become a specialized tool through its program. Alan Turing reached a related idea from mathematical logic: a universal machine could simulate any effective symbolic process when given an appropriate description. Their work made programmability, not a machine’s fixed physical purpose, central to the idea of a computer.

Different projects explored different technical compromises. The Atanasoff-Berry Computer used electronic binary arithmetic for linear equations but retained fixed control. Bletchley Park’s Bombes tested Enigma settings electromechanically, while Colossus used vacuum tubes to process encrypted teleprinter text at electronic speed. Vannevar Bush’s differential analyzer and the M-9 gun director represented equations through analog mechanisms and feedback. Claude Shannon connected Boolean logic to relay circuits, George Stibitz built binary relay calculators, and Norbert Wiener generalized feedback and control as cybernetics.

ENIAC brought electronic speed to a large, programmable decimal calculating system. Its plugboards and switches could configure a new problem, but rewiring took days. Human computers—often women using mechanical calculators—continued to formulate steps, choose branches, and check results. Wartime urgency supplied funding and applications, while secrecy determined which achievements entered public knowledge or moved into commerce. These machines demonstrated both the power of electronic calculation and the need for a faster way to deliver instructions.

### Stored programs create a software industry

[[Stored-Program and Mainframe Computing]] begins with placing instructions and data in one high-speed memory. This solved ENIAC’s programming bottleneck and allowed programs to be copied, generated, and manipulated as data. The Manchester Baby demonstrated the principle in 1948, EDSAC turned it into a useful university service, and commercial machines such as UNIVAC and the IBM 701 brought the architecture into government, science, and business.

Mainframe computing organized expensive machines around continuous utilization. Operators mounted tapes, fed card decks, managed printers, and queued batch jobs; programmers waited for printed results outside the computer room. This arrangement suited stable workloads such as billing and payroll but made experimentation slow. SAGE added interactive displays, communications, and a deliberately human decision point to an air-defense system, while also revealing how hardware, software, and test-data errors could produce dangerous false alarms.

The general-purpose machine elevated software to the same importance as hardware. Grace Hopper and other pioneers developed systems that assembled reusable routines. Compilers then translated familiar symbolic notation into new machine code. Fortran made scientific programming accessible without sacrificing much performance, and COBOL pursued portable business programs across vendors. Operating systems automated the housekeeping once performed by operators. IBM’s System/360 family used a shared architecture to preserve software across machines of different sizes, turning compatibility into a powerful commercial strategy.

### Solid-state manufacturing changes the scale

[[Semiconductor and Integrated Circuit History]] explains how vacuum tubes gave way to devices whose switching occurred inside solid material. Bell Laboratories’ 1947 transistor eliminated hot filaments and vacuum enclosures, but more than a decade of materials science and manufacturing was needed before it transformed computers. Engineers learned to purify crystals, introduce P-type and N-type regions through doping, shift from germanium to silicon, grow stable oxides, and define microscopic structures with photolithography.

The integrated circuit treated a whole circuit as one manufactured object. Jack Kilby demonstrated that active and passive components could share a semiconductor wafer; Robert Noyce combined that insight with Jean Hoerni’s planar silicon process and deposited interconnections. Printed circuits and wartime miniaturization supplied important precedents. Clean rooms, documented production, and yield management made the microscopic designs reproducible rather than merely possible.

Government procurement created the first large market. Minuteman missile guidance and Apollo spacecraft demanded circuits that were small, light, rugged, and reliable, helping producers improve their processes and reduce cost. Around Fairchild Semiconductor, engineers moved among new firms and spread knowledge through what became Silicon Valley. Intel began with memory, and its 1103 chip helped displace hand-assembled magnetic cores. Moore’s law summarized the resulting density trend, but institutions, demand, and manufacturing skill were as important as the physics.

### The microprocessor brings ownership

[[Microprocessors and Personal Computing]] addresses the commonality problem created by increasingly complex chips. A custom circuit with more functions served fewer customers, making mass production risky. The microprocessor provided a general processor whose behavior could be specialized through software. Intel’s 4004 and successors worked with memory, input-output, and support chips, while microcode helped realize useful instruction sets within early density limits.

Manufacturers first saw processors as components for embedded controllers and sold development kits to engineers. Hobbyists instead recognized complete small computers. The Altair’s 1975 magazine appearance triggered a market, and communities such as the Homebrew Computer Club shared designs around affordable processors including the MOS 6502. The KIM-1, Apple I, Apple II, Commodore machines, and many competitors translated a semiconductor component into personal ownership.

Software made the hardware valuable. Microsoft’s BASIC let Altair owners program at a higher level. VisiCalc turned the Apple II into a business tool, while Lotus 1-2-3 did the same for the IBM PC. IBM’s use of outside processors and Microsoft software enabled compatible clones. Microsoft’s right to license MS-DOS across those machines shifted power toward the software platform. Personal computing thus emerged from both electronics and a social desire for direct, unrestricted access.

### Interaction becomes a defining property

[[Interactive Computing and Human Interfaces]] traces a parallel challenge to batch computing. J. C. R. Licklider imagined human-machine symbiosis in which computers extended thought through rapid response. Project MAC explored multiple access and machine-aided cognition, while time-sharing switched one processor among many terminals quickly enough to give each user an illusion of private control.

Wesley Clark took another route with the LINC, placing a small computer directly beside a laboratory user. DEC’s PDP-1 and PDP-8 extended this hands-on model through transistorized minicomputers and open information that customers used to build specialized systems. Interactive computing changed the program from a submitted artifact into an environment that a person could explore moment by moment.

Xerox PARC integrated personal workstations, bitmapped displays, the mouse, windows, icons, WYSIWYG documents, laser printing, and Ethernet in systems such as the Alto. Direct manipulation connected hand movement with visible digital objects. Ethernet let capable workstations share files and services at high speed, turning time-sharing’s passive terminal into the client of a distributed system. The history shows that user interfaces are not decoration: they determine whether technical capacity becomes useful human action.

### From ARPANET to an Internet

[[Internet Infrastructure History]] follows the convergence of computing and communication. ARPANET connected research machines through packet switching, which divided messages into independently addressed units rather than reserving a dedicated circuit. Packet routing allowed links to be shared and supported communications that could continue around failures.

As the network grew, its protocols changed. The 1983 transition to TCP/IP separated reliable end-to-end streams from best-effort packet routing and allowed heterogeneous local networks to interconnect. Unix distributions carried the protocols into laboratories, Ethernet connected local users, and networks such as BITNET and Usenet brought academic and social communities into the larger system.

The National Science Foundation connected supercomputer centers and created a high-speed backbone for general research. Regional networks attached to it, and commercial mail services and private carriers pressed against the acceptable use policy. Legal and administrative changes eventually permitted broader purposes, and by 1995 commercial entities operated the backbones. Governance moved among defense, science, commerce, and specialized bodies because names, addresses, standards, and interconnection are institutional resources as well as technical ones.

### The Web organizes an open network

[[World Wide Web and Online Services]] distinguishes the Internet from the information system that runs over it. Before the Web, modem users reached bulletin boards and centralized services such as CompuServe, the Source, Prodigy, and America Online. These environments established messaging, forums, databases, curated content, advertising, and online identity, but each largely operated as its own hub.

At CERN, Tim Berners-Lee and Robert Cailliau combined URLs, HTTP, and simple HTML with hypertext. A URL could point anywhere, HTTP moved resources across unlike machines, and HTML made linked publication accessible. Mosaic added a polished graphical interface, while Netscape extended browsers with secure transport and cookies. Encryption supported commerce; cookies supported sessions and preferences while also enabling surveillance.

The Web’s flatness made every address equally reachable but supplied no universal index. Yahoo began as a human-curated directory and grew into a portal. AltaVista emphasized fast automated search, while Google ranked pages partly through their incoming links and paired the method with a restrained interface. The dot-com era commercialized these capabilities, but the enduring system arose from open standards combined with many competing services.

### Computing moves into pockets and communities

[[Mobile and Social Computing History]] completes the convergence. Cellular networks divide territory into reusable radio cells and hand sessions from tower to tower as a person moves. Wi-Fi supplies local wireless access through interoperable standards. BlackBerry made mobile e-mail compelling, the Palm Pilot refined a simple digital organizer, and the Handspring Treo combined organizer and phone more coherently.

Apple’s iPhone and iPad joined touch interaction, applications, cameras, positioning, motion sensors, media, cellular service, and Wi-Fi around a microprocessor. These devices are universal computers receiving and producing many kinds of bit streams, yet the networks they use remain divided by coverage, pricing, carrier control, and technical policy. Convergence does not erase institutional boundaries.

Online communities also converged. Weblogs made publishing easier, Twitter built a medium around brief mobile messages, and Facebook centralized identity, relationships, news, and media within a persistent platform. These systems descend from bulletin boards, chat rooms, and Usenet as much as from ARPANET. Modern networked computing arose when bottom-up social use met top-down research infrastructure.

Across this history, no single invention determines the outcome. Digital representation supplies common technical glue, solid-state electronics expands capacity, and convergence creates new combinations. Yet compatibility, procurement, regulation, business models, communities, and interface design decide which possibilities take hold. Computing advances when machinery becomes programmable, networked, and accessible enough for people to make it part of their lives.
