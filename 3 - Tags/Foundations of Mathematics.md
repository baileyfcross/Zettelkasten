# Foundations of Mathematics

Parent topic: [[Mathematics]]

Foundations of Mathematics is the chapter-level topic for the book's connected treatment of elementary algebra, analytic geometry, polynomial equations, trigonometry, functions, limits, differential calculus, Euclidean geometry, and spherical geometry. Full Notes link to the focused child topics rather than directly to this page.

## Overview Chapter

Mathematics becomes coherent when its subjects are understood as layers of one language rather than as isolated collections of formulas. The foundations in this chapter begin with real numbers and legal algebraic operations, turn those numbers into coordinates and functions, and then use limits and derivatives to describe change. Plane geometry supplies the figures that motivate many equations, while vectors and spherical geometry extend the same reasoning into space. Each later topic depends on earlier conventions about equality, order, measurement, and proof.

### Numbers and algebraic structure

[[Real Number Algebra and Arithmetic]] establishes the line on which real quantities are ordered. [[Rational Number]]s describe integer ratios, [[Irrational Number]]s fill the remaining positions, and [[Interval Notation]] records connected solution sets. [[Absolute Value]] converts signed position into distance from zero. The [[Laws of Real Number Arithmetic]] explain why expressions may be regrouped or distributed, while the [[Order of Operations]] removes ambiguity from symbolic notation. [[Scientific Notation]], [[Prime Factorization]], and [[Exponent Laws]] then provide practical forms for scaling, divisibility, and powers.

[[Algebraic Manipulation and Inequalities]] turns those laws into solution techniques. A [[Rational Expression]] carries domain restrictions inherited from its denominator, and a [[Rational Exponent]] links powers with roots. Transformations must preserve the original problem: [[Equivalent Equations]] have the same solutions, but a nonreversible step may create an [[Extraneous Solution]]. [[Partial Fraction Decomposition]] reduces a [[Proper Rational Function]] to simpler terms. Inequalities require additional care because multiplication by a negative number reverses order; [[Linear Inequality]], [[Quadratic Inequality]], and [[Rational Inequality]] problems therefore produce intervals, while a [[Two-Variable Inequality]] produces a region.

### Coordinates, equations, and functions

[[Cartesian Analytic Geometry]] translates geometric position into ordered pairs. The [[Cartesian Coordinate System]] gives equations a visible form. [[Slope of a Line]] measures directional change, and the [[Linear Equation of a Line]] packages that change with a point or intercept. Slope relations identify [[Parallel and Perpendicular Lines]], while the [[Distance Between Points]] and [[Perpendicular Bisector]] connect coordinates to Euclidean constructions. The [[Circle Equation]] and broader [[Conic Section]] equations show how entire curves can be encoded. Vectors add direction and magnitude; the [[Dot Product]] measures angular alignment and proves identities such as the [[Parallelogram Law]].

[[Polynomial Equations and Factorization]] studies algebraic expressions whose variable powers are nonnegative integers. A [[Linear Equation]] has one first-degree solution, while [[Completing the Square]] leads to the [[Quadratic Formula]] and the [[Quadratic Discriminant]] classifies its roots. For a general [[Polynomial]], [[Polynomial Long Division]] produces a quotient and remainder. The [[Remainder Theorem]] evaluates the special remainder from division by a linear factor, and the [[Factor Theorem]] identifies roots with linear divisors. The [[Rational Root Theorem]] narrows possible rational roots, while the [[Fundamental Theorem of Algebra]] guarantees complete linear factorization over the complex numbers.

[[Function Analysis and Transformations]] makes dependence itself an object of study. A [[Function]] assigns exactly one output to each input in its [[Domain and Range|domain]], and the [[Vertical Line Test]] recognizes that condition on a graph. The [[Absolute Value Function]], [[Exponential Function]], [[Rational Function]], and [[Piecewise-Defined Function]] illustrate different domains and graph shapes. [[Function Composition]] joins rules in sequence; a [[Function Transformation]] shifts, scales, or reflects a graph; and an [[Inverse Function]] reverses a one-to-one mapping.

### Trigonometry and local change

[[Plane Trigonometry]] begins by measuring rotation through [[Angle Measure in Radians]]. On the [[Unit Circle]], coordinates become the sine and cosine [[Trigonometric Ratios]], and repeated rotations explain [[Trigonometric Periodicity]]. The [[Pythagorean Trigonometric Identity]] follows directly from the circle equation. [[Angle Addition Identities]] combine rotations and generate the [[Double-Angle Identities]] and [[Half-Angle Identities]]. For general triangles, the [[Law of Sines]] relates opposite parts, while the [[Law of Cosines]] extends the Pythagorean theorem beyond right angles.

[[Limits and Continuity]] supplies the transition from finite algebra to calculus. The historical [[Method of Exhaustion]] anticipates the [[Limit of a Sequence]], where successive approximations approach a fixed value. A [[Limit of a Function]] describes nearby behavior even when the function value at the point differs or is absent. Its two [[One-Sided Limit]]s must agree. [[Continuity at a Point]] additionally requires that common limit to equal the function value. The [[Intermediate Value Theorem]] guarantees that a continuous graph passes through every intermediate height. [[Horizontal Asymptote]] and [[Vertical Asymptote]] describe limiting end or boundary behavior, and the [[Squeeze Theorem]] determines a difficult limit from simpler bounds.

[[Differential Calculus]] converts limits into rates of change. The [[Derivative]] is the limiting slope of secant lines, and the [[Derivative Function]] records that rate across a domain. [[Tangent Line Approximation]] explains differentiability as local linearity. The [[Power Rule]], [[Product Rule]], and [[Quotient Rule]] differentiate common algebraic forms, while the [[Chain Rule]] handles composition and [[Leibniz Notation]] keeps the nested dependencies visible. The [[Derivative of Exponential Function]] and [[Derivative of Logarithmic Function]] reveal the special reciprocal behavior of inverse functions $e^x$ and $\ln x$.

### Euclidean and spherical geometry

[[Euclidean Geometry Theorems]] organizes plane reasoning as definitions and proofs. [[Euclidean Axiomatic Geometry]] makes assumptions explicit, after which basic results such as equality of [[Vertical Angles]] and the [[Triangle Angle Sum]] support longer deductions. The [[Isosceles Triangle Theorem]] and [[Triangle Congruence]] transfer equality among corresponding parts. The [[Pythagorean Theorem]] links right triangles to squared lengths, [[Parallelogram Theorems]] organize parallel quadrilaterals, and [[Similar Triangles]] preserve shape under scaling. Circle geometry culminates in the [[Inscribed Angle Theorem]] and the supplementary-angle property of a [[Cyclic Quadrilateral]].

[[Spherical Trigonometry and Spatial Vectors]] shows which plane intuitions survive in three dimensions and which must change. A [[Plane Equation in Three Dimensions]] is controlled by its normal vector, and a [[Sphere Equation]] fixes distance from a center. A [[Great Circle]] is cut by a plane through that center; three of its arcs bound a [[Spherical Triangle]]. The [[Cross Product]] produces a perpendicular vector and area, while the [[Scalar Triple Product]] produces oriented volume. Normals determine the [[Angle Between Planes]], making vector methods suitable for spherical angles. The [[Spherical Law of Cosines]] and [[Spherical Law of Sines]] solve general spherical triangles, and [[Napier's Rules]] provide a compact method for right spherical triangles.

Together, these subjects describe a progression from quantity to structure, from structure to representation, and from representation to change. Algebra makes transformations accountable, coordinates make geometry computable, functions make dependence explicit, limits control approximation, derivatives describe local variation, and geometry keeps the symbolic results tied to space. The recurring principle is preservation: every valid calculation or proof must preserve the relationship being studied while expressing it in a form better suited to the next question.
