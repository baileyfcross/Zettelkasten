# Finite Element Analysis

Parent topic: [[Computational Science and Simulation]]

Numerical approximation of structural, thermal, fluid, dynamic, and electromagnetic continuum problems through interconnected elements and nodal field variables.

## Overview Chapter

Finite element analysis converts a continuum problem into a finite system of algebraic equations. A physical domain is divided into elements that meet at nodes, and each node carries a defined set of unknown field values. An interpolation model describes how those nodal values vary inside an element. Local equations relate the unknowns to loads, sources, material behavior, and geometry; assembly then combines every local contribution into a global system. Boundary conditions make the model correspond to the physical problem, and the solved nodal values support recovery of secondary results such as stress, heat flux, velocity, or electromagnetic field intensity.

The algebra behind this process appears in [[FEM Mathematical Foundations]]. Matrices organize element coefficients, nodal variables, and load vectors, while partitioning separates groups such as free and prescribed degrees of freedom. Addition drives assembly, multiplication applies interpolation and constitutive relationships, and transposition forms symmetric energy expressions. Determinants, inverses, Gaussian elimination, and eigenvalue problems provide conceptual routes to static and dynamic solutions. In realistic computation, the structure of a large sparse global matrix matters as much as its formal closed-form solution.

[[Finite Element Method Foundations]] describes the recurring workflow across engineering domains. Discretization selects element size, type, and arrangement. Nodes define degrees of freedom, and shape functions interpolate the unknown field. An element stiffness or characteristic matrix and an element load vector encode local behavior. Assembly maps them into a global equation by shared node numbers. Essential boundary conditions may be imposed through elimination, penalty terms, or multipoint constraints. Once the primary field is solved, derivatives and constitutive equations produce the quantities engineers actually interpret.

Structural examples begin with [[Axial Bar Finite Elements]]. An axially loaded member develops normal stress and strain linked by Young's modulus. A two-node element uses linear displacement interpolation and constant strain, whereas a three-node element uses a quadratic field that better represents distributed loads or changing cross section. The strain-displacement matrix turns nodal displacement into element strain, and equivalent nodal loads represent distributed or thermal actions. Varying-area bars demonstrate why subdivision or higher-order interpolation is necessary when stiffness changes along a member.

[[Truss Finite Elements]] extend axial behavior to networks of oriented members. A planar truss element has two translations at each endpoint, but its physical deformation remains axial. Direction cosines project local extension and force into global coordinate components. Each oriented element matrix is assembled at its endpoint degrees of freedom, so shared joints enforce compatibility and equilibrium. After free joint translations are solved, member stress and support reactions can be recovered and checked against independent joint-equilibrium calculations.

[[Beam Finite Elements]] represent flexure rather than pure axial extension. A planar beam node carries transverse deflection and slope, allowing the interpolation to reproduce both displacement and rotation. The element stiffness matrix relates these degrees of freedom to shear forces and bending moments through elastic modulus, section moment of inertia, and length. Simply supported and cantilever boundaries prescribe different combinations of displacement and slope. Solved deflections lead to internal shear, moment, and bending-stress results.

Thin or longitudinally constrained bodies require two-dimensional continuum models. [[Plane Stress Finite Elements]] distinguishes plane stress from plane strain through the constitutive matrix. Constant-strain and linear-strain triangles, linear quadrilaterals, and isoparametric elements offer different accuracy and geometric flexibility. A plate with a circular hole illustrates symmetry reduction and stress concentration: a quarter model can represent the full structure when symmetry constraints are correct, but the mesh must resolve the steep gradient around the hole. Von Mises stress provides a compact post-processing view, while equilibrium and analytical stress-concentration factors help validate it.

The same assembly logic applies beyond structural displacement. [[Finite Element Heat Conduction]] uses temperature as one scalar degree of freedom per node. Conductivity and geometry form the element heat-conduction matrix, while internal generation and prescribed heat flow contribute load terms. Convection adds both a boundary coefficient and an ambient-temperature contribution. One-dimensional elements can model composite walls; two-dimensional meshes handle interacting boundary conditions and lateral gradients. The solved temperature field is differentiated to recover heat flux.

[[Finite Element Fluid Flow]] focuses on ideal potential flow. Incompressibility, inviscid behavior, and irrotationality reduce the flow to a scalar velocity potential or stream function. Streamlines show the instantaneous velocity direction, while potential derivatives give velocity components. Fluid elements assemble nodal potentials under boundary conditions around bodies such as cylinders and airfoils. This formulation is deliberately distinct from viscous flow, where shear, boundary layers, and no-slip behavior require a richer set of governing equations.

Time-dependent structure introduces [[Finite Element Dynamics]]. A mass matrix represents inertia and a damping matrix represents energy loss in addition to elastic stiffness. Modal analysis solves a generalized eigenvalue problem to obtain natural frequencies and mode shapes. Harmonic response analysis studies sinusoidal forcing near those frequencies, while transient analysis follows response to an arbitrary load history. Boundary conditions, density, mesh resolution, time step, and forcing representation all affect the reported motion.

Electromagnetic problems first require the physical framework in [[Electromagnetic Field Foundations]]. Maxwell's equations and the continuity equation connect fields to charge and current, while constitutive relations introduce permittivity, permeability, and conductivity. Static, quasi-static, source-free, and time-harmonic assumptions produce different simplified equations. Interface conditions determine which tangential and normal components are continuous or discontinuous. The Lorentz force connects fields to mechanical action, and the Poynting vector represents electromagnetic power flow and energy balance.

[[Finite Element Electromagnetics]] then turns that theory into a discretized field solution. Linear line and triangular potential elements approximate scalar fields and their gradients. Variational and weighted-residual formulations derive element equations for Poisson, Laplace, and Helmholtz problems. Waveguide analysis becomes an eigenproblem whose eigenvalues approximate cutoff parameters and whose eigenvectors approximate modal fields. Automatic mesh generation reduces manual preparation, higher-order elements improve approximation through additional nodes, and absorbing boundary conditions permit finite meshes to represent radiation toward an unbounded exterior.

Across every chapter, finite element analysis is an approximation discipline rather than a software ritual. Element selection, mesh resolution, coordinate and unit consistency, material assumptions, and boundary conditions determine what the equations mean. Analytical solutions, equilibrium, conservation, symmetry, and refinement studies provide independent checks. A credible result is therefore not merely a converged solver output; it is a discretized model whose assumptions and numerical behavior have been tested against the physics it claims to represent.
