## Overview Chapter

Internet memes are not simply popular pictures or jokes. They are families of related digital artifacts whose creators know about one another and preserve some combination of shared content, form, and stance while introducing variation. A single clip that accumulates millions of views may be viral without becoming a meme; a meme becomes visible when people imitate, recaption, reenact, remix, or otherwise transform a recognizable model. This distinction places creative participation at the center of digital culture rather than treating audiences as passive endpoints of transmission.

[[Internet Meme Concepts]] provides the vocabulary needed to analyze that participation. The meme began as an analogy between cultural and biological replication, emphasizing variation, selection, and retention. Competing schools then located memes in mental representations, observable behavior, or any information copied through imitation. For Internet research, the most useful unit is a group of related artifacts rather than an invisible idea or an isolated file. Comparing content, form, and stance shows exactly what a derivative preserves and changes. Mimicry recreates a performance; remix edits or combines existing material. Together they produce a hypermemetic environment in which visible copying and alteration become ordinary modes of communication.

[[Viral and Memetic Diffusion]] explains how those artifacts travel and why some invite more participation than others. Digital systems improve longevity, fecundity, and copy fidelity through storage, rapid duplication, search, and large networks, but perfect copying can coexist with extensive variation. Popularity lies on a spectrum: viral items concentrate attention on one version, founder-based memes retain a prominent original alongside derivatives, and egalitarian memes distribute attention across many instances. Positive or highly arousing emotion, packaging, prestige, positioning, and participation opportunities can encourage viral sharing. Memetic success also depends on simplicity, humor, accessible tools, and producerly gaps that give participants something to complete, parody, or repair. Network hubs create broad exposure, while bridges carry artifacts between communities.

[[Meme Genres and Vernacular Creativity]] shows how recurring conventions make large-scale creativity possible. Genres are not merely visual categories; they are recognizable ways of performing communicative actions. Reaction Photoshops recontextualize a reusable figure, photo fads organize embodied imitation, flash mobs coordinate public performance, and lipdubs use prerecorded music as a scaffold for group identity. Misheard lyrics and recut trailers expose how sound and editing redirect meaning. Image macros combine recurring images and short captions, stock characters turn predictable personas into social classification systems, and rage comics use a shared vocabulary of faces to narrate everyday experience. Competent participation requires meme literacy: creators must preserve enough of a formula for recognition while changing enough to make a meaningful contribution.

[[Political Memes and Digital Activism]] connects these cultural practices to persuasion, mobilization, and public discussion. Political memes can advocate a cause, coordinate grassroots action, or provide formats for ordinary people to express and debate positions. Their templates support connective action by linking personalized stories without imposing a single organizational voice. Occupy's 99 percent photographs joined diverse accounts of hardship through a common structure, while 53 percent countermemes reused that grammar to dispute its interpretation. Such exchanges produce polyvocal discourse rather than ideological unanimity. Memetic photos, culture jamming, and censorship-evasion wordplay let participants reframe elite images and official narratives, although the democratic value of any meme depends on who can speak, circulate, and interpret it.

[[Meme Globalization and Localization]] follows shared formats across linguistic and cultural boundaries. Users contribute to globalization by translating jokes, copying visual formulas, and remaking media for their own networks, but circulation is selective. Language-specific humor and dense cultural references resist translation, while visually simple formats may travel more easily. Even apparently universal images retain local meanings. Glocalization therefore works by preserving a portable grammar and substituting recognizable people, settings, stereotypes, or political concerns. A translation can also change stance, turning approval into critique or shifting who is addressed. Gangnam Style remakes and localized image macros demonstrate how creators use a global template to express national, institutional, subcultural, and personal identities.

Across these five topics, the central analytical move is to study relationships among versions. Counts of views or shares describe exposure, but they do not reveal whether people copied, transformed, challenged, or localized an artifact. Content, form, and stance reveal continuity; genre reveals the rules participants recognize; network structure reveals pathways of diffusion; and political or cross-cultural context reveals unequal power and situated meaning. Internet memes are therefore best understood as communicative processes: collections of artifacts through which people negotiate belonging, humor, identity, authority, and public life.
