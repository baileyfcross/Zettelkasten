# Life Science Data Analysis with R

Parent topic: [[Health Data Analysis]]

Life science data analysis combines statistical reasoning, matrix methods, reproducible computation, and biological context to extract reliable evidence from complex measurements. Full Notes should use one of the focused child topics below rather than linking directly to this chapter tag.

## Overview Chapter

[[R Statistical Computing and Data Wrangling]] establishes the reproducible workspace in which the analysis occurs. R packages extend the language, data frames hold rectangular observations, projects and working directories organize files, and delimited text formats keep inputs portable. Explicit import steps and readable dplyr transformations preserve the connection between raw measurements and derived results. This foundation matters because an inferential result cannot be audited when the code, package state, file paths, or data-selection steps are ambiguous.

[[Statistical Inference and Resampling]] connects observed samples to claims about populations. Random variables and sampling distributions describe why estimates change, while null distributions and p-values quantify how surprising a statistic would be under a skeptical model. The central limit theorem and t-distribution support common approximations, and confidence intervals combine effect magnitude with uncertainty. Power calculations anticipate detectable effects, while Monte Carlo and permutation procedures let computation replace or test analytical approximations when their assumptions are doubtful.

[[Exploratory and Robust Data Analysis]] examines distributional shape and relationships before a formal model is trusted. Cumulative distributions, quantiles, histograms, box plots, scatter plots, and quantile-quantile plots expose scale, asymmetry, outliers, and departures from assumed distributions. Stratification can reveal relationships concealed by aggregation. Robust summaries and rank-based methods reduce the influence of extreme observations, while careful graphic design prevents chart form, scale, or decorative dimensions from obscuring the underlying measurements.

[[Matrix Algebra for Statistical Models]] supplies a compact language for many observations and predictors at once. Vectors represent measured outcomes, matrices encode predictors, and multiplication expresses simultaneous linear combinations. Transposes, inverses, rank, and systems of equations explain when least-squares estimates exist and whether they are unique. The normal equations show the mathematical target, while QR decomposition provides a more stable computational route. Matrix notation therefore connects a statistical model's scientific meaning with the numerical operations used to fit it.

[[Linear Model Design and Contrasts]] focuses on how scientific comparisons become columns in a design matrix. Indicator variables encode group membership, the intercept defines a reference condition, and coefficients describe specified differences. Main effects and interaction terms distinguish shared effects from effects that vary across groups. Contrasts turn fitted coefficients into the exact comparisons investigators want, while analysis of variance compares nested models. Collinearity and confounding reveal designs in which parameters cannot be separated, emphasizing why balanced experiments matter before computation begins.

[[High-Dimensional Inference and Multiple Testing]] addresses experiments that test thousands of features simultaneously. A conventional p-value controls uncertainty for one test, but repeating the procedure creates many opportunities for false positives. Family-wise error control uses stringent thresholds such as Bonferroni correction, whereas false-discovery methods tolerate some errors within a useful candidate list. The Benjamini-Hochberg procedure, positive false discovery rate, null-proportion estimation, and q-values offer different operating summaries, while p-value histograms and volcano plots expose the joint pattern of effects and uncertainty.

[[Count Models and Empirical Bayes]] adapts statistical models to discrete and heterogeneous biological measurements. Binomial and Poisson distributions describe counts under different event regimes, and likelihood connects observed values to parameter estimates. Gamma and F distributions model positive, right-skewed quantities such as variances. High-throughput experiments also provide many related features from which a hierarchical model can borrow information. Empirical Bayes variance moderation stabilizes noisy feature-specific estimates and improves the power of moderated tests without pretending that all genes share exactly the same variance.

[[Distance Geometry and Dimension Reduction]] turns high-dimensional samples into a geometry that can be compared and visualized. Euclidean distances form pairwise relationships, but thousands of coordinates prevent direct plotting and can include substantial noise. Singular value decomposition rotates the data into ordered directions of variation, and low-dimensional projections approximate the dominant structure. Principal components, variance-explained summaries, and multidimensional scaling then reveal sample relationships, possible outliers, or experimental structure while making explicit what information the reduced representation retains.

[[Statistical Learning and Validation]] shifts emphasis from interpreting individual parameters to predicting outcomes for new observations. Hierarchical clustering and heatmaps reveal unsupervised grouping, while conditional expectation and Bayesian classification describe optimal prediction targets. Training and test sets separate model construction from evaluation, and cross-validation uses repeated held-out folds to select hyperparameters without rewarding overfitting. K-nearest neighbors illustrates how neighborhood size changes flexibility, while bin smoothing and LOESS estimate unknown smooth relationships from local groups of observations.

[[Batch Effects and Latent Factor Adjustment]] addresses systematic technical variation that can imitate or hide biology. Processing dates, laboratories, reagent lots, or other batches become dangerous when they are confounded with the outcome of interest. Exploratory correlations and principal components can reveal this structure. Known batches may be included in a linear model, but unrecorded influences require latent factors or surrogate variables. Adjustment must protect the biological contrast explicitly, because blindly deleting dominant components can remove the desired signal along with unwanted variation.

Together, these topics form an end-to-end method for data-intensive life science. Reproducible code preserves the evidence trail; inference and exploration establish what the measurements support; matrix and linear-model reasoning encode comparisons; multiple-testing and empirical Bayes methods exploit high-throughput structure; and dimension reduction, learning, and batch correction reveal patterns without confusing technical artifacts for biology. The recurring discipline is to align every computational step with the scientific question and the data-generating process.
